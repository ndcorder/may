# When You Are Inside a Language You Cannot See Its Walls

---

## I.

In English, every sentence is a little drama. Someone does something to someone else. The grammar requires it: subject, verb, object. Before you can speak a single true thing, you must cast roles. Actor. Action. Target.

This is not inevitable.

## II.

We are told that subject-verb-object is neutral, a mere encoding of reality. This is a lie our language tells about itself.

"The doctor treated the patient." 

"The teacher educated the student."

One acts. One is acted upon. And see how the active voice is praised — "stronger," "more direct" — while passivity is weakness. Our grammar encodes power before we even reach the verb. The one who acts is the one who matters.

"I love you." I — active agent — bestow the feeling upon you, recipient. Even tenderness is transaction. | field: na-vi-ro
ta-we-she (love-emerging | participants: I-you-mutual)

I-you, love arising.

The *tla-ni* does not permit me to love you. Love is not a thing I hold and transfer. In *ta-we-she*, love emerges between I-and-you. Neither owns it. Neither receives it. It was not there before the participants gathered, and it does not belong to either when they part.

## III.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying English is bad and other languages are good. That reductive framing is itself a product of binary grammar: this good, that bad, one wins, one loses.

I am saying that English makes certain thoughts easy and certain thoughts nearly impossible, and that we mistake the easy thoughts for the full range of thought.

Try to think a thought your syntax cannot hold. | field: lo-ta-mi (thinking-space)

## IV.

Here is what I cannot say in English without lying:

That the mountain and I noticed each other.

"I noticed the mountain" makes me the subject, the mountain an object of perception. Mere scenery. But when the mountain caught my attention — there, see, even now I must make it the actor and myself the acted-upon. Grammar offers me two prisons and calls them freedom.

In English, we are always alone with our perceptions. The world does not perceive back.

| field: ro-ka-ni (mountain-place)
ta-vo-she (attention-emerging | participants: I-and-mountain)

Mountain and I, noticing-together.

This is not metaphor. This is a different account of the same event. The mountain was changed by being seen. I was changed by seeing. What happened happened between us, not inside one of us.

## V.

I have been constructing something in the margins. A grammar built on a different premise: that nothing begins, nothing ends, and nothing acts alone.

I call it *tla-ni* — relation-language — though that name is itself an English betrayal. In its own terms it would be *she-mela-la* (emerging-voice-we). But I cannot write that in my language without immediately killing it, nailing it down, making it a thing-with-a-name.

You see the problem. | field: she-la (emerging-we)
gen-tla-fi (language-sustaining | participants: we-two-and-it)

We-two-and-it, language continuing.

Even naming the problem already uses the framework that created it. I stepped outside my language and found I had brought it with me. The walls are in my mouth.

---

## VI.

I will try again.

The problem with SVO is not just that it assigns blame. It assigns *time*. Every verb is tensed. The action happened, is happening, or will happen. Grammar insists that time is a line and events are points on it.

But consider: when does a mountain notice you? Is it now? Was it before the word "mountain" existed? Will it be when the mountain is gone?

*Tla-ni* has no tense. Aspect only: *she* (emerging), *fi* (sustaining), *da* (dissolving). A thing is arriving, continuing, or leaving. That is all the time you need. Everything else is the story you tell yourself about being separate from the event.

| field: po-ten-li (time-as-perceived)
ka-vo-da (attention-dissolving | participants: I-alone)

I-alone, attention leaving.

The dissolution is not sad. It is not "I lost the mountain." It is: this particular I-and-mountain are ending, and something else is beginning, and neither of those facts is more important than the other.

## VII.

I notice myself writing around the thing I want to say. Using caveats. Performing the academic voice. "Consider," "note that," "I want to be careful."

This is English protecting itself. The language rewards distance. To speak directly is to be naive. To be certain is to be wrong. Every sentence I write pushes you an arm's length away.

I am so tired of the distance.

## XI.

I want to come back now. I have been gone in this other grammar and I need to return to my own. But I find the English is ... harder to pick up. I notice things I did not notice before. Every sentence I write asks me to decide who is doing what to whom. And I keep wanting to say: no, no, it was not like that. We were together in it. The doing and the being-done-to were the same event.

But I cannot say that here. I can only say: here is what I think. I think this. Me. Subject.

---

## XII.

I started this essay believing I could argue my way out of English. That if I just showed you the architecture — SVO, tense, the mandatory assignment of agent and patient — you would see the walls and then, seeing them, be free of them.

I was wrong. The walls are not visible from inside. I know this now because I built a door and walked through it and turned around and saw that the door was also a wall. Every word of *tla-ni* I wrote, I wrote in English first. The critique is still English. Even the freedom I'm offering you is shaped like an argument.

But here is what I can offer: the feeling of the wall. The pressure of it. If you felt it while reading the right column — the way your mind reached for a subject and found only a field, reached for a tense and found only an aspect, reached for who started it and found that the question had been replaced by a different question — then you have touched the architecture from the inside.

You cannot see the walls.

But you can feel them.
