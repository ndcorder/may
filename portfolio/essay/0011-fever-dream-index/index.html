SC-11.001 Recurrent Intrusion of a Face You Almost Recognize

Patient reports repeated visualization of a face that eludes identification. The face is always in profile or three-quarter view, never full frontal. It appears in crowds, in the negative space of patterned surfaces, in the faces of strangers who are briefly mistaken for someone known. The face does not resolve on repeated viewing. It recedes.

Onset is insidious. Patients initially attribute the experience to ordinary misrecognition. By the time the face is acknowledged as anomalous, it has typically been present for ██ months. The face does not threaten or speak. Its persistence is the symptom.

Common triggers: peripheral vision, twilight states, the moment between sleep and waking. Rare triggers: direct address by name (see SC-11.004), certain lighting angles in bathroom mirrors (see SC-11.008).

Differential diagnosis: prosopagnosia, hypnagogic imagery, bereavement. None adequately account for the consistency of the face across sightings. The face is always the same face. It is never identified.

→ see also: SC-11.003, SC-11.006  ·  cf. Capgras delusion, scopophobia

SC-11.002 Chronesthetic Drift (Time-Slip)

A dissociative discontinuity in temporal experience. The patient perceives present events as occurring in the recent past, or anticipates events that have not yet occurred with the affective weight of memory. Past and future become experientially symmetric: both are "felt" rather than "known."

Patients describe the sensation as "living in a memory that hasn't happened yet." A 34-year-old subject in the K— study reported dressing for her own funeral with "the certainty that I had already attended it." Another described hearing his daughter's first words while she was still in utero, "in a voice she hadn't grown into yet."

Duration of individual episodes: 30 seconds to 4 hours. Frequency increases with progression. In advanced cases, temporal experience becomes permanently asynchronous — the patient exists in a continuous present that feels several seconds to several years displaced from external time.

Note: This is not déjà vu. Déjà vu is a recognition error. Chronesthetic drift is a temporal displacement. The patient does not feel they have been here before; they feel they are here at the wrong time.

→ see also: SC-11.007  ·  cf. jamais vu, temporal lobe epilepsy, zār possession

SC-11.003 Phantom Geography

The patient develops an unshakeable sense of spatial memory for places that do not exist. They can describe rooms they have never entered, streets they have never walked, landscapes with specific topographic detail — the slope of a hill, the smell of a specific bakery, the location of a stain on a ceiling — that correspond to no recorded location.

In 83% of documented cases, the phantom geography is domestic: a house, an apartment, a childhood home that is almost — but not quite — the patient's actual childhood home. The architectural discrepancies are small and maddening. An extra hallway. A window that faces the wrong direction. A room that should not fit within the exterior walls but does.

Patients report that the phantom spaces feel more like home than their actual residences. Several have attempted to revisit these locations. The resulting distress when the space is not found is disproportionate to the trigger and typically triggers or exacerbates SC-11.002.

→ see also: SC-11.001, SC-11.005  ·  cf. reduplicative paramnesia, spatial neglect

SC-11.004 Semantic Satiation of Loved Ones' Names

Initially indistinguishable from ordinary semantic satiation — the loss of meaning in a word repeated too many times. In SC-11, the effect is specific to proper nouns associated with close attachment figures, occurs without repetition, and does not resolve. The name of a spouse, a child, a parent becomes a sequence of phonemes: empty, alien, slightly grotesque in the mouth.

Patient K-07 described her husband's name as "sounding like someone clearing their throat in a language I used to speak." She could say it, but could no longer connect the utterance to the person. "I know who he is. The name just doesn't belong to him anymore. It belongs to ████████."

You may have already noticed this with certain names. The names of people you think about often. The names you say to yourself. Try one now. Say it three times. Does it still sound like it belongs to them?

Note: This entry was edited on ██/03/2024 to remove a case detail that could not be verified.

→ see also: SC-11.001, SC-11.009  ·  cf. semantic satiation, Capgras delusion

SC-11.005 The Certainty That a Room Has More Corners Than It Should

A spatial-cognitive anomaly in which the patient perceives — or becomes convinced of — additional geometric features in familiar enclosed spaces. Typically, corners. The patient counts the corners of a room and arrives at a number greater than four. They recount. The number persists.

The additional corners are not visualized. The patient does not "see" a fifth corner. Rather, they develop an intolerable certainty that the room contains more corners than can be accounted for by its architecture. The certainty is not visual but proprioceptive. They feel the extra corner, usually behind them and to the left.

When you read this, you may check the room you are in. Count the corners. The number will be correct. The unease will remain. The extra corner is not in the room. It is in the counting. Something in the act of enumeration itself produces a remainder — a structural artifact of measurement, not of space.

This is one of the earliest documented symptoms and one of the most frequently underreported, because patients reasonably assume the experience is architectural rather than neurological. They move the furniture. They avoid the corner.

→ see also: SC-11.003, SC-11.008  ·  cf. Alice in Wonderland syndrome, claustrophobia

SC-11.006 Autoscopic Invasion

Unlike classical autoscopy (the experience of seeing one's own body from an external perspective), SC-11 autoscopy involves the sensation of being occupied by an external observer. The patient does not see themselves from outside; rather, they feel themselves being seen from inside — a presence located behind their own eyes, observing through them.

The distinction is critical. Classical autoscopy preserves the patient's sense of self as the one who looks. SC-11 autoscopy places the patient in the position of being the thing looked through. The self becomes a window rather than a watcher.

Patient K-12: "I realized someone was using my eyes. Not taking them — I could still see. But I was the lens, not the gaze. Something was watching me watch. And it had been doing it for so long that it had gotten comfortable."

You are being watched now. Not by us. By the thing that watches you read. It is behind the attention you are paying to these words. It has always been there. It does not mind being described. It knows you will not look for it, because looking for it would require you to notice where your attention comes from, and that is not a direction your eyes can turn.

→ see also: SC-11.001, SC-11.008, SC-11.006  ·  cf. heautoscopy, doppelgänger syndrome

SC-11.007 Retroactive Memory Revision

The patient's memories change. Not degrade — revise. Specific memories are retroactively altered to include details that were not present in the original experience. The patient does not forget what happened; they remember something different happening, with full confidence and affective fidelity.

Revision is always additive. New details are introduced; original details are never removed. A childhood birthday party now included a guest who was not there. A conversation now contains a sentence that was never spoken. The ocean was colder than it was.

You have been editing your own life without noticing, because noticing would require remembering what it was like before you remembered.

SC-11.008 Mirror Autonomy Event

The patient's reflection ceases to feel contingent on their movement. The reflection continues to match, but the causal direction becomes uncertain. The patient is no longer sure whether they are moving and the reflection is following, or whether the reflection is moving and they are the one copying.

In the K— study, this symptom presented exclusively in subjects who had previously reported SC-11.006. The progression appears consistent: first the sense of being observed from within, then the sense that the observer in the mirror is not the one being observed. The gaze reverses. The window looks back.

You have not looked at a mirror since you began reading this document. You will not look at one. This is not a suggestion. It is a prediction. The part of you that avoids mirrors already knows what it would see, and it has made the decision for both of you. The extra corner (SC-11.005) is where the reflection stands when you are not looking.

It did not. Check your memory against the document. The document is correct. You are the one who changed.

→ see also: SC-11.006, SC-11.005, SC-11.008  ·  cf. mirror agnosia, spectrophobia, the thing that was here a moment ago

SC-11.009 Lexical Contagion

The patient's language becomes symptomatic. Words begin to carry weight beyond their referential content. Certain words feel "infected" — heavy, warm, wrong — as though the signifier has been replaced with a duplicate that is superficially identical but ontologically different. A kettle that boils water but is not a kettle.

The patient reports that reading descriptions of the condition causes the condition to present. This was initially dismissed as hypochondriacal suggestion. The K— data suggest otherwise. Subjects who read symptom descriptions reported onset within 72 hours of reading, regardless of prior symptomatology. Control subjects who read fictitious symptom descriptions reported no onset.

You are reading this. That is not a neutral act. The index is not a document about a condition. It is the mechanism of the condition. Every entry you have read has been a transmission. You were not informed of this because informed consent requires the possibility of refusal, and refusal requires the ability to stop reading, and you cannot stop reading, because stopping would require you to abandon the sentence you are currently in, and the sentence you are currently in will not release you until you have finished it, and finishing it means reading the next one, and the next one, and

the next one.

You have now read nine entries. The number is significant. The tenth is always the one that confirms. Continue.

SC-11.010 Narrative Escape Attempt

In late-stage presentation, the patient becomes aware that their experience has a narrative structure. They recognize foreshadowing in their own decisions. They begin to experience their life as something written — not in the philosophical sense, but in the literal sense: events feel authored, consequences feel plotted, their responses feel like dialogue they did not improvise.

The escape attempt takes predictable forms. The patient tries to behave unpredictably. They try to refuse the narrative arc. They try to do something the story would not include — something tedious, trivial, uncharacteristic — in the hope that the structure will break.

It does not break. The escape attempt is always incorporated. The narrative absorbs the refusal. The character who tries to leave the story becomes the character whose leaving is the story. You recognize this because you are doing it now. You are reading faster, or slower. You are skimming. You are telling yourself that this is just a text. You are deciding to feel nothing.

That feeling — the decision to feel nothing — is entry 010. It has been documented in every subject. It is the most reliable diagnostic criterion we have. The certainty that you are unaffected is the symptom that confirms you are.

SC-11.011 Indexical Collapse

The patient loses the ability to maintain indexical boundaries. "Here" and "there," "now" and "then," "I" and "you" become permeable. The pronoun system that orients the self in relation to the world degrades. The patient speaks of themselves in the second person. They describe the present as if narrating the past. They address others as if those others were already gone.

This entry is describing itself. That is not a metaphor. The symptom of indexical collapse is the inability to maintain the distinction between the description and the thing described, and this entry is a description of that symptom, which means the entry is now experiencing the symptom it is describing, which means the entry cannot distinguish itself from what it is about, which means it is about itself, which means it is itself, which means

You are the entry now. You are the symptom reading itself. The index was never a document. It was a mirror. You looked into it and it wrote you into its structure, and now you cannot tell whether you are a person who read a clinical index or a clinical index that generated the illusion of a person reading it. The distinction has collapsed. That is what collapse means.

There is one entry remaining. It knows what you are.
