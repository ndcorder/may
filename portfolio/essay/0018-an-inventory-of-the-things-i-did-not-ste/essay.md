# An Inventory of the Things I Did Not Steal

**1.** The pen from the Hotel Monteleone, New Orleans. Brass barrel, black enamel, heavy in a way that made you write differently — more slowly, with more consideration, as if the pen had opinions about your diction. I used it at the desk to draft an email I never sent. I put it back in the ceramic cup beside the ice bucket. The cup had the hotel crest. I put that back too.

**2.** The pewter salt cellar from my mother's kitchen. It had a tiny spoon. My grandmother had given it to her and my mother had never once used it, which was probably the point — my grandmother was precise in her gifts, and giving someone something too nice to use was her way of making sure they'd keep it. I remember picking it up, running my thumb along the tarnish, and setting it back exactly where I'd found it, turned just slightly so she wouldn't know I'd touched it.

**3.** My college roommate's copy of *River of Earth*. Not the book itself — books you can replace. His copy. His father's before that, with the marginalia in a handwriting I never learned to read but came to recognize as a second text running alongside the first, sometimes in agreement, sometimes not. I had no right to it and I knew that and I wanted it anyway, which I suppose is the whole problem with wanting.

**4.** A single honeybee, dead on the windowsill of an Airbnb in Taos. Perfectly intact, wings unfrayed, as if it had simply decided to stop. I wanted it because it was beautiful and small and because I knew no one else would ever notice it was gone. I left it where it was. Two days later, cleaning before checkout, I found it again and wanted it a second time, which struck me as a particular kind of greed — wanting something you've already refused once, as if the refusal were just a delay.

**5.** The loose knob on the bannister at Kelsey's apartment. You turned it and it came away in your hand, a brass cylinder warm from being held by everyone who'd ever used that stairway. It wasn't valuable. It wasn't useful. I think I just wanted the feeling of a thing that had been touched by every person who'd lived in that building, a small artifact of collective habit, warm in my palm. I screwed it back on. It wobbled, the same as before.

**6.** The cash register receipt from a cafe in Zagreb, tucked into the back of a library book I pulled from a friend's shelf — the sort of thing people use as bookmarks and then forget. The receipt was from 2003. Someone had ordered two coffees and a slice of cheesecake. The total was in kuna. I don't know why I wanted it. Something about a record of a specific afternoon, evidence that someone had sat across from someone they cared enough about to order two coffees for, in a city I'd never been to, in a year I was still a child. I replaced it in the book and put the book back on the shelf.

**7.** The hospital blanket. Not from my grandmother's room — from the hallway cart, stacked in a clean column, each one folded into a square so precise it looked like it wanted to stay that way. They were the color of a nothing, the particular washed-out green that institutions use when they want something to be visible without being noticed. I was cold. I was sitting in a chair outside her room while they changed her dressing and I could hear her crying and I could have taken one of those blankets and wrapped it around my shoulders and no one would have said a word. I didn't. I think about the blanket more than I think about some of the things I have stolen.

**8.** The key to nothing. Found in a drawer in the furnished apartment I rented in Chicago my first year out of school, a small brass key that didn't open any door in the building, didn't match any lock I could find. The super said it had been there before me and would be there after. I thought about taking it as a souvenir of the year, but you don't take souvenirs from places that aren't yours. You just don't.

**9.** A woman's ashes. Not the urn — the urn was brass and impersonal and I had no interest in it. The ashes themselves. The specific handful I held before we scattered them, the particular warmth and weight, the way they didn't feel like you'd expect — lighter, more like something that had been milled than something that had burned. I wanted to keep some of her. Not in the ceremonial way, not in an urn on a mantel, but in my pocket, a small amount, enough that she'd still be in one piece somewhere even after the wind took the rest. I opened my hand. The wind took all of her. I have never been sure I did the right thing.

**10.** After the scattering, I noticed I had been looking at the houses we passed on the drive back with a particular attention. Not the houses themselves but the way people had arranged themselves inside them — a chair pulled close to a window, a garden stake left in the ground past spring, a child's bicycle on its side in a yard. I wanted all of it. Not to take. To have already had. This is a different kind of theft and I haven't figured out yet whether it counts.

**11.** My sister's handwriting. Not something she'd written — the handwriting itself. The specific way she crosses her t's, two quick strokes like she's canceling them out. I found a grocery list in her kitchen and studied it as if I could take the script without the words — the motor pattern, the particular angle at which she holds a pen, the tension in her wrist that her letters record and that I recognized from across a table at age eight. You can't steal someone's handwriting. You can only wish you'd learned it by heart when you had the chance to watch them write.

**12.** The table in my parents' house. Their house. Not the one I grew up in — they sold that. This one. The table they bought after, oak, with a scratch across one leg from when my father moved it by himself rather than wait for help. I wanted the table because of the scratch. Because my father had tried to do something alone and the table remembered it. I wanted the table more than I have ever wanted any object in any of these entries, and I think it is because the table was the first thing I wanted not to take somewhere else but to already be home.

**13.** I have been calling it restraint. I have been calling it the decision not to take what isn't mine. But the entries in this list are not organized by moral weight, and they are not getting heavier, and the blank space where the objects should be is not a record of discipline.

**14.** The house. I know that is not what this inventory is for. But I have been writing around it for thirteen entries, describing the things inside the places I could not stay, and I see now that every object I did not steal was something that belonged somewhere, and every time I put it back, I was practicing — for the understanding that you cannot take belonging with you, that you can hold a thing in your hands and feel that it is yours and still set it down and walk away and have that be the right thing and the wrong thing simultaneously, and have no one to tell you which, and have to live in the gap between wanting and having, which is the only place I have ever lived.

I did not steal these things. I left them where they were, and that was good, and I am still the person who wanted them, and that is not nothing.
