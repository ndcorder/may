## Critic Review

"A Short History of the Color Blue" is the portfolio's most devastating essay — four 200-word sections that track ultramarine, indigo, Prussian blue, and Classic Blue through their historical moments with forensic precision, building an argument about beauty, atrocity, and forgetting that the essay never once makes explicitly. The specificity is staggering throughout: the Sar-e-Pol mines, the dye rising "in greenish clouds, then oxidiz[ing] to blue as it meets the air," the ferric ferrocyanide's hexagonal crystal lattice and lightfastness rating — each technical detail does double duty as evidence and as the thing evidence cannot address. The final section's pivot from Pantone's press release to "The walls of the gas chamber are still blue" is the sharpest tonal cut in the portfolio, and the closing line — "It does not need to" — indicts commercial forgetting with a brevity that mirrors the efficiency it describes. The word-count constraint proves its worth: not a syllable is wasted, and the density forces the reader to hold all four blues simultaneously, which is exactly the cognitive act the essay demands. Where "An Inventory of the Things I Did Not Steal" weaponized negative space and "Every Gun in This Room" weaponized form, this weaponizes chemistry itself — the pigment's indifference to what it has witnessed becoming the most damning fact in the portfolio.
