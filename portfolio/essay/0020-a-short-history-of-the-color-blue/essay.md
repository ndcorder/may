The painter grinds the stone. Lapis lazuli: from the Sar-e-Pol mines in Badakhshan, carried by mule across the Hindu Kush, loaded onto dhows in Persian ports, unloaded in Venice, sold to a color merchant who sells it to the workshop of Fra' Giovanni da Fiesole. Now the painter holds it. A raw chunk of blue in his palm. He breaks it, heats it, grinds it again against a stone slab, then kneads the powder into pine resin and a little linseed oil to make the paste. His fingers are stained for weeks. The dust rises with his breath. In San Marco, the cell walls wait. Fra' Giovanni has been commissioned to paint the Annunciation in Cell 3: Gabriel arriving, Mary flinching, a loggia receding into gold leaf and blue sky. The painter works the blue into the vaulted ceiling above Mary's bowed head, thinning it with egg yolk where it must glow and applying it thicker where the light is raw, in the places where the chapel window will strike the wall at the morning hour. Ultramarine: from beyond the sea. The Virgin's mantle costs more per ounce than the painter's annual wages. He uses it anyway.

Her fingers are blue. Not her fingertips—the stain has pushed past the nail beds and into the creases of her knuckles, the folds beneath her rings. The longer fingers on her left hand, which reached deeper into the vats. In the cooling shed behind the indigo works, she presses her weight against the wooden paddle, submerging the bundled leaves for the fourth time today. The dye rises in greenish clouds, then oxidizes to blue as it meets the air. The smell is sharp enough to taste. She has been told this crop brings four shillings per pound in Liverpool. She will not see those shillings. What she sees is the color, how it finds her hands no matter how she scrubs with lye and pumice. She lost two children last August. Fever. The doctor who came said the marsh air was unwholesome. She knows the smell of the vats has the same sweetness. The blue stays. The dye masters in Charleston call it the truest indigo they have seen. She calls it nothing. She carries it in her skin.

The walls are blue. Not painted: stained. The pigment has bonded with the plaster at the molecular level, iron cyanide compounds forming where hydrogen cyanide gas met the iron oxide already present in the cement. The reaction produces ferric ferrocyanide. Prussian blue. The guards did not choose this color. The gas chose it. In the ruins of the disinfection chamber at Majdanek, a chemist scrapes a sample from the wall. In his laboratory he will confirm what the stain already testifies: the concentration of hydrogen cyanide exceeds any threshold for delousing, is consistent only with the threshold for killing. The blue is brightest in the corners where the gas pooled. He photographs the walls. The chimney did not burn the sky; it functioned efficiently. There is no metaphor adequate to the efficiency. In his notes, the chemist records the ferric ferrocyanide, its hexagonal crystal lattice, its lightfastness rating. The color will outlast the building. It has already outlasted the women who were undressed in this room. It does not know what it has witnessed. It holds nothing. It is permanent.

Pantone 15-4020. Classic Blue. Instilling calm, confidence, and connection. The press release says: a timeless and enduring blue hue, elegant in its simplicity. Suggestive of the sky at dusk. Leatrice Eiseman, executive director of the Pantone Color Institute, calls it a color that highlights our desire for a dependable and stable foundation. It is the blue of hospital walls, of corporate identity, of a meditation app loading screen. It is the blue of the banking app on your phone. You trust it. You were designed to trust it. In promotional materials, a woman in a Classic Blue sweater stands on a balcony at evening. The sky behind her is cerulean. The dye merchants in Charleston are dead. The chambers at Majdanek are a tourist site with a visitor center. The altarpiece in San Marco is behind glass now, attended by a guard who asks you not to use flash photography. Classic Blue is available in household paint, nail polish, cotton bedding. The walls of the gas chamber are still blue. Zyklon B has been rebranded as a fumigant. Classic Blue. The press release does not mention any of this. It does not need to.
