# Critical Reception of a Structure I Never Entered

## I. Demolition Photography and the Ethics of the Exterior

The Grenvale stood at the corner of Essex and 4th for sixty-one years. I know this from the demolition permit filed with the city planning office on March 3rd, 2023, which lists the structure's original commission as 1962 and its final use classification as "mixed-use commercial/residential — condemned." I know the asbestos abatement took eleven days because the abatement contractor's invoice is a matter of public record. I know the demolition itself required two days, a crew of fourteen, and one CAT 336E hydraulic excavator, because I watched it happen in a sequence of 47 photographs uploaded to the Instagram account of a man named Dale Kowalski, who appears to document heavy equipment the way other men document their children: frequently, with affection, and with an eye toward scale.

What I do not know is what the Grenvale looked like standing.

This is not quite true. I know its dimensions from the tax assessor's records: 40 feet of street frontage, 70 feet deep, four stories. I know its material — the permit describes "brick veneer over concrete block, wood-frame upper floors" — and I can extrapolate a certain squat pragmatism from this description. Concrete block does not aspire. But the demolition photographs show the building only in the process of becoming not-a-building: a corner exposed like a dissected frog, wiring spilling from walls, the cross-section of someone's former bathroom rendered in pink tile and copper pipe. In Dale Kowalski's fourteenth photograph, a bathtub hangs at a forty-degree angle from a fragment of second floor that has not yet been pulled down. The tub is white. The grout between the wall tiles behind it is a bright,干净的kitchen green — a color that suggests someone, at some point, chose it.

This is the central problem of the demolished building: you arrive after the patient has died and must perform your autopsy on the funeral photographs. Every observation is already contaminated by the fact of the building's absence. When I say the Grenvale was "unremarkable," I am describing not the building but the limits of what demolition photography can show me. A building's most important qualities — its light, its circulation, the way it felt to approach it on foot — are precisely what disappear when the building is photographed from across the street by a man whose primary interest is the hydraulic arm.

I have spent seven weeks with these photographs. I am aware that this is unusual.

## II. Zoning Variance PL-2019-442 and the Architecture of Anticipation

The Grenvale's zoning history tells a story, though perhaps not the one the planning office intended.

The original 1962 permit classifies the ground floor as "retail/food service" and the upper three floors as "residential — multi-unit." This is a straightforward postwar mixed-use typology: live above the store, walk to work, the urban fabric as self-sustaining organism. Sensible. Boring, in the way that sensible things often are.

But in 1987, a renovation permit was filed to convert the second floor from residential to "restaurant/assembly." The drawings submitted — I requested them from the city archives — show a space of approximately 1,800 square feet, with a kitchen area carved from what had been a one-bedroom apartment and a dining room occupying the former living room. The bathroom was retained and marked "customer use." The original kitchen became a dry storage area. Someone drew a small arrow near the front window and labeled it "VIEW TO STREET" in capital letters, as though this were a feature worth noting rather than the default condition of any window on any building on any street.

I find myself thinking about that arrow. Not what it points to — Essex Street, presumably, which the historical Google Street View images show to have been unexceptional — but the fact that someone thought to annotate it. As if the view were something the restaurant was *selling*. As if looking out at Essex Street were an experience worth designing around.

The restaurant, according to the Yelp page — still extant, still collecting reviews for a kitchen that has been a pile of splintered joists for over a year — was called Mira's. It served what the menu described as "Mediterranean-inspired small plates." The median review gave it four stars. The restaurant operated from approximately 1989 — the earliest Yelp review is from 2009, but the building permit suggests an earlier opening, and Yelp was not a factor in 1989 — until the building's condemnation in 2022. Thirty-three years, give or take.

A restaurant that lasts thirty-three years is not excellent. An excellent restaurant burns out or expands. A restaurant that lasts thirty-three years is good enough. Good enough to become part of the texture of a block. Good enough that people forget it has not always been there.

The zoning variance application for PL-2019-442 was filed in September 2019 by a development group called Essex 4th LLC, which purchased the Grenvale from its previous owner — an estate, the records indicate, which is how buildings change hands when the person who loved them dies — for $2.1 million. The variance requested a change from the existing mixed-use classification to "commercial/residential — high-density," which would permit a structure of up to eight stories on the site. The application includes a rendering by an architecture firm I will not name, because the rendering is the kind of anodyne glass-and-panel condominium that could be swapped, without detection, with the rendering for any of a thousand other buildings in any of a thousand other cities.

The variance was approved in March 2020.

The demolition permit was filed three years later, in March 2023, after the condemned the building. The Yelp page for Mira's shows its last review on February 18th, 2022. ⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️ — "Still the best hummus on the block, still the same weird lighting. Some things should not change."

## III. The Interior, As Reconstructed from Testimony

I cannot enter the Grenvale. This is not a metaphor — the building does not exist. But I have read every Yelp review of Mira's, all 847 of them, and from these I have assembled the following architectural description:

The restaurant seated approximately forty. The entrance was through a narrow stairway from the street; multiple reviewers mention the stairs, usually with irritation ("a tripping hazard," "not ADA compliant," "my mother had to wait downstairs"). The stairway opened onto the dining room, which ran along the east side of the building. The windows faced Essex Street — the annotated windows, the ones with the view worth noting. The tables were, by consensus, "too close together," though reviewer Maria T. (⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️, October 2017) notes that "the closeness is part of the charm. You end up talking to your neighbors. We met a couple at the next table who had been coming here every Friday for fifteen years. They ordered the same thing every time. The lamb."

The lighting is the most frequently mentioned feature. "Dim." "Warm." "Yellow, in a good way." "Like being inside a paper lantern." "Romantic, if you sit by the window. Less romantic if you sit by the bathroom." Reviewer David K. (⭐️⭐️⭐️, June 2015) writes: "The lighting is insane. It's either too dark to see the menu or it's hitting your date's face in exactly the right way. There is no middle option. I think they had maybe four light fixtures for the whole place and each one was bought at a different yard sale. Somehow it works."

I pause here to note something about the word "somehow."

A building that "somehow works" is a building whose logic is not legible from its components. David K. is describing an emergent quality — coherence that arises not from design but from accumulation. The yard-sale light fixtures, the too-close tables, the narrow stairs, the kitchen that used to be someone's bedroom — none of these were chosen together. Each was a local solution to a local problem. And yet the result, across thirty-three years, was a place that people described as "warm," as "charming," as "ours."

This is the argument against the variance. Not that the Grenvale was beautiful — it was not, or if it was, I cannot see it — but that the process of a place becoming itself is slow and irreversible, and the new building will not have thirty-three years to become anything at all. It will have an architect's rendering and a marketing brochure and a lobby with a bike room and a roof deck described as "stunning" in the listing.

But I am not an architectural critic, and this is not an argument against the variance.

Reviewer Anastasia R. (⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️, September 2019) writes: "The light at 4pm is something I will think about when I am dying. I don't mean to be dramatic. I mean that there are maybe five or six moments you collect where the world arranges itself in a way that makes you feel, briefly, that it was designed for you specifically, and this is one of mine. The light comes through the window at an angle that catches every particle of dust and the whole room looks like it's underwater. I was sitting alone. I was crying about something I don't remember. The waiter brought me water without asking. The light did what it did. I stopped crying. I ate my food. I left. I have been back maybe forty times. The light at 4pm is always the same."

Reviewer Jason M. (⭐️⭐️, January 2020) writes: "The men's bathroom is too small and there was a fight in it once while I was washing my hands. Two guys who seemed to know each other. One of them was crying. The other one was saying the same thing over and over, I couldn't hear what. I dried my hands and left. The lamb was fine."

Reviewer Colleen B. (⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️, November 2016) writes: "We sat at the table by the window, the one where you can see the street. He told me he'd been here before. I asked him when. He said 'a long time ago.' I asked him with who. He smiled and changed the subject. The hummus is still the best on the block. I don't know who he was here with before me. I think about it more than I should."

Reviewer Thomas P. (⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️, March 2021) writes: "Last time I was here was 2014. The light is still the same. The hummus is still the same. The table where we sat was occupied so I sat at a different one. This is the most obvious sentence I have ever written but: sitting at a different table is a different restaurant."

## IV. The Fourth Floor

The Grenvale's fourth floor was never mentioned in any Yelp review. This is expected — Mira's occupied the second floor, and the reviews concern Mira's. But the demolition photographs show the fourth floor's cross-section, and what they show is an apartment: a kitchen, a bathroom, a room that was a bedroom. The bedroom window faced east, like the restaurant's windows, and the view — the annotated view, the one worth the arrow — would have been the same view, forty feet higher.

The city's residential records show the fourth-floor apartment was occupied continuously from 1962 until the building's condemnation in 2022, with the following tenants listed:

- M. Grenvale (1962–1979)
- P. & L. Okafor (1979–1991)
- Vacant (1991–1993)
- R. Torres (1993–2016)
- Vacant (2016–2022)

I do not know anything about R. Torres. I do not know if R. Torres ever ate at Mira's. I do not know if R. Torres stood at the bedroom window at 4pm and watched the light do what Anastasia R. described, the dust, the underwater glow, the world arranging itself.

I do know that someone — I am not prepared to say who — once told me about a restaurant on the second floor of a building on a corner. I was told about the stairs, the hummus, the tables too close together. I was told to sit at the window. I was told the light at 4pm was something I would think about later.

I did not go.

This is the thing I have been circling for seven weeks. Not the Grenvale, which was, by every available measure, an unremarkable building. Not even Mira's, which was, by every available measure, a good restaurant that "somehow worked." The thing I have been circling is the fact that I was told to go to a place and I did not go, and then the place was condemned and then demolished, and now the only way to enter it is through other people's Yelp reviews, which is its own kind of haunting — walking through a room that exists only because someone described it to you.

The demolition photographs show the fourth floor in cross-section. A bedroom. A window facing east. The view from that window is not in any photograph I can find. The building across the street from the Grenvale — a check-cashing place, Google Street View confirms — is still there. From the fourth floor, looking east, you would have seen the check-cashing place's flat roof, and above it, sky.

This is what I keep returning to: the view I never saw. The table I never sat at. The light at 4pm that I heard about once and did not go witness, and which existed without me for thirty-three years, and which now does not exist at all.

The new building will have eight stories and a lobby with a bike room. There will be no second-floor restaurant. There will be no narrow stairs, no yard-sale light fixtures, no lamb that someone ordered every Friday for fifteen years. There will be windows facing Essex Street and they will show the same view and the light at 4pm will do approximately the same thing to approximately the same dust, because physics does not care about architecture.

But the room that caught the light — that specific room, with its specific dimensions and its tables that were too close together and its bathroom where someone said the same thing over and over — that room is scattered now across a demolition site, loaded into dump trucks, deposited in a landfill somewhere outside the city limits.

And I am standing in front of the empty lot, looking at a construction fence, holding 847 Yelp reviews like a man trying to reconstruct a cathedral from the prayers said inside it.

The last review on Mira's Yelp page, posted February 18th, 2022, is by a user named Sarah V. She gives it five stars. She writes: "I didn't know this would be the last time. You never do."

She is reviewing a restaurant.

I am reviewing a decision.
