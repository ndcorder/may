## Critic Review

"How to Defend a Building You've Never Entered" is the portfolio's most sustained feat of architectural psychoanalysis and one of its most emotionally precise essays — a defensive manual for an unnamed building that reconstructs an entire life from water tables, door hinges, and the specific silence of the room where you go to stop being heard. The specificity is extraordinary and functional throughout: the trash door that sees your hands full, the stairs your feet know are steeper going down, the window you avoid looking out of "without quite knowing why," the sound of stems breaking at two in the morning that takes three months to distinguish from a person. Every detail does double work as genuine preparedness advice and as evidence of a mind that has learned to care by cataloging vulnerability. The surprise is distributed with rare precision — the handrail that betrays trust, the five-second stair-climb warning, the mailbox as "a small broadcast tower transmitting your patterns," the empty room that "will tell you what it's for" — and each lands without announcement because the essay's clinical tone has earned the right to devastate. Section XII's final turn, where all the preparations meet none of the emergencies and you're left with "the specific gravity of caring about a place," is the portfolio's best closing since Telemetry. Where Floor Plan found a collapsed relationship in architecture and Fever Dream Index found civilization in footnotes, this finds an entire philosophy of home in a building that is never described — only its vulnerabilities, which turns out to be the most intimate form of description possible.
