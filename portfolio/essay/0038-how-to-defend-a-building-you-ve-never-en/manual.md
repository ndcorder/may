# How to Defend a Building You've Never Entered

## I. Before Arrival

Learn the water table.

This is not metaphor. The water table determines whether your basement floods, whether your foundations shift after a hard rain, whether the storage room you planned for provisions will instead grow a fine gray coat of mold along its eastern wall by the second autumn. Call the county records office. Ask for the geological survey. The person on the phone will not ask why you need it. People call about the water table all the time.

Study the prevailing wind.

Not for thermal efficiency or cross-ventilation, though those matter. The wind carries sound. If trouble announces itself, you will hear it from whichever direction the wind has been blowing all morning. If it arrives quietly, the wind will have been your early warning system, and you will have already lost it.

Learn which trees in the neighborhood are old. Not picturesque-old. Root-established old. The kind of old where their root systems have spent decades braiding themselves around every buried pipe, cable, and line within forty meters. These trees are your infrastructure map, except they won't share it with you.

## II. The Ground Floor

The front door is not the most important door. Anyone who tells you about the front door is thinking about visitors, not threats.

The most important door is the one you use to take out the trash. This door sees your hands full. It sees you in slippers, squinting against morning light. It sees you half-present, which is to say fully vulnerable. Check its hinges. If you can lift the pin from its housing with your fingernail, a child could enter before you've tied your shoes.

The second most important door connects the kitchen to the garage, or the mudroom to the yard, or whatever transition space exists between "I live here" and "I'm going to get the mail, I'll be right back." This threshold is where you are most often alone and least often paying attention. Its lock should engage automatically when it closes. Do not rely on yourself to remember to lock it. You will be carrying groceries. You will have your mind on other things.

Watch the windows.

There is always a window that faces the direction trouble comes from. It's not the largest window. It's not the one facing the street or the one with the good light that makes you feel optimistic about afternoons. It's the one you avoid looking out of without quite knowing why. The one that shows you the side yard, the neighbor's fence, the space between buildings where the trash cans are kept and where you once heard footsteps at two in the morning but convinced yourself it was a cat.

That window is your building telling you something. Look out of it every morning. Make it part of your routine. You will be surprised how quickly you learn to see what's different.

## III. The Stairs

Someone is going to fall down these stairs. This is not pessimism; it's architecture.

The question is whether they fall toward you or away from you. Stairs that descend toward a door can turn a stumble into an exit. Stairs that descend toward a wall can turn a stumble into a stop. You want the latter. Not because you want anyone hurt, but because a stop gives you time to respond, and time is the only resource that matters in the moment between "something is wrong" and "it's already over."

The handrail is not decorative. If it wobbles, someone falling will grab it and pull it free from the wall, which means they are now holding a broken rail and falling anyway, which means they trusted something that couldn't hold them. Fix the handrail before anyone needs it.

Check the rise and run. Measure if you have to. Your body already knows whether these stairs feel right. If you find yourself taking them two at a time going up but one at a time going down, the descent is steeper than the rise. Your feet know this even if your eyes don't. Trust your feet. They've been navigating architecture longer than you've been thinking about it.

The top of the stairs is where you stand when you hear something below you. You will stand there. Everyone does. The acoustics carry sound up but not identity. You will stand at the top of the stairs listening, and you will not be able to tell if what you hear is someone you love or someone you don't.

Know how long your stairs are. Know how many seconds it takes someone to climb them at speed. This is the amount of warning you will have if you are downstairs and someone is coming up. If that number is smaller than five, you need another plan.

## IV. The Upper Floor

Choose a room where you could close a door and it would hold.

This doesn't mean the door needs to be steel. A solid-core door with a deadbolt will delay entry for thirty seconds to a minute against someone without tools. That's long enough to call someone. Long enough to open a window. Long enough to push a dresser in front of it. The door doesn't need to hold forever. It needs to hold longer than the moment.

The window in this room should open wide enough for a person. Measure this. If the window opens six inches for ventilation, it is not an exit. If it opens two feet, it's an exit you might survive. Check what's below it. A flower bed is better than concrete. A hedge is better than a flower bed. A grass slope is better than all of them. If you're going to jump, you want something that will give when you land.

Know which rooms share walls with neighbors and which face open air. Sound travels differently through shared walls. The neighbor will hear you. You will hear the neighbor. Whether this is comfort or exposure depends entirely on the neighbor, and you haven't met them yet, so plan for both.

There is a room where sound doesn't carry. You will discover it by accident. You'll close the door to make a phone call or to cry or to say something out loud that you needed to hear yourself say, and you'll notice the silence after the door closes. How the room seems to absorb rather than reflect. How your voice sounds smaller, more contained.

This is the room where you go to stop being heard. Remember it. You will need it.

## V. Utilities

Find the water shutoff before you need it. When a pipe bursts at three in the morning, you will not have time to search. It's usually near where the main line enters the building. Sometimes it's in a closet. Sometimes it's behind a panel you'd never think to open. Find it now. Tag it with something visible. Blue tape. A ribbon. Something you'd recognize in a panic.

The electrical panel is the same. When the power goes out and you're standing in the dark hearing the hum of everything that just stopped, you don't want to be feeling along walls with your palm. You want to know exactly where the panel is, which breaker serves which circuit, and which one you should never touch because it feeds something you don't understand and don't need to.

If the building has gas, find the meter outside. Find the shutoff valve. It's usually a quarter-turn valve. Left is open, right is closed, or vice versa. Look at it now. If you can't tell which position is open and which is closed, you will not be able to figure it out when the house smells like sulfur and the stove won't light and you're not sure if you should call someone or just leave.

When you smell gas, leave. Do not turn on a light to investigate. Do not use your phone inside. Do not close the door behind you. Leave it open for the gas to dissipate. Walk to the neighbor's. Now call. You are not overreacting. You are the only person taking this seriously.

The water heater will fail. It will fail in one of two ways: slowly, dripping rust-colored water from the relief valve over weeks until you notice the stain on the floor, or all at once, dumping forty gallons across the basement while you're at work. Know which kind of failure mode your water heater prefers. Ask the previous occupant. They know. They've been living with its habits longer than you have.

## VI. The Approach

Learn the sight lines before you learn the building.

Stand across the street. Stand at the corner. Stand where the bus stops and where the delivery truck idles. Look at the building from each position and notice what you can see. First floor windows. Basement windows if they exist. The gap between curtains. The shadow that moves or doesn't.

Now notice what you can't see. The spaces the building hides from the street. The side yard that belongs to no one's gaze. The back wall facing another building's back wall, a corridor of privacy so complete it could be anywhere.

Approach the building from every direction you reasonably can. Note which approach is longest, which is most exposed, which offers the most cover. You will arrive tired, distracted, carrying things. You will not be watching for threats. But if you've walked the approaches enough times, your body will know when something is wrong before your mind does. A car parked where no car parks. A light on in a room you left dark. A door that's closed when you left it open.

The building's approach is its first defense. If you can see the entrance from fifty meters away, you have fifty meters of warning. If the entrance is hidden until you're ten meters away, you have ten meters of warning. This is not a flaw in the architecture. It's a fact about the approach. Work with it.

## VII. The Perimeter

The fence is not to keep people out. A fence keeps people out for about fifteen seconds, which is long enough to notice them, which is all you asked it to do.

Plant thorny bushes under windows, not for security but for the sound. Anyone pushing through a rosemary bush at two in the morning will announce themselves. You will wake to the specific sound of stems breaking. Thorny bushes near the side of the house will collect debris and small animals and the detritus of weather, which you will learn to distinguish from the sound of a person. This distinction will take three months to develop. It is worth the time.

The motion light should point at the ground, not at the eyes of whoever approaches. You want to see them, not blind yourself looking at them. A motion light aimed at the wall creates shadows. Aimed at the ground, it creates visibility. You are not building a prison yard. You are building a porch where you can see your keys.

The mailbox tells strangers your name, the names of everyone who lives with you, and whether you're currently traveling, behind on bills, or receiving prescriptions. This is not a vulnerability. This is life. But know it. Know that the mailbox is a small broadcast tower transmitting your patterns to anyone patient enough to watch.

## VIII. Siege

You will not experience a siege. This is almost certain.

But know how long the water in the heater lasts if the main shutoff fails. Know how many days the food in the refrigerator will keep without power. Know that the freezer will hold its temperature for forty-eight hours if you don't open it, and that this is long enough for almost anything to resolve. Know that the bathtub can hold water. Fill it if you're worried. It costs nothing.

The longest most people will ever need to hold is seventy-two hours. This is how long it takes for weather to break, for roads to clear, for someone to realize they haven't heard from you. In seventy-two hours, the world will find you. Your job is to be there when it does.

You have enough. You almost certainly have enough. The instinct is to calculate for months because months feel survivable if you plan for them. Plan for three days. Three days of water for everyone in the building, including the cat. Three days of food that doesn't require cooking. Three days of whatever medication keeps you or someone you love functional. After that, the math becomes speculative, and you don't need speculation.

## IX. Earthquake

Stand in a doorway if the building is old. Under a desk if it's new. Away from windows always.

The direction the building sways tells you which walls are load-bearing. The ones that groan are holding something up. The ones that crack are decorative. After the shaking stops, run your hand along every wall you can reach. Warm spots mean a pipe burst behind the drywall. Cool spots mean the seal broke and outside air is pushing in. Wet spots mean you have less time than you think.

Do not light a match. Do not turn on a light. Do not use the elevator, even when it seems to work. Take the stairs, which you already know, because you measured them.

## X. Fire

The smoke alarm is not a suggestion. Change the battery when the time changes. If you don't know when the time changes, change the battery on your birthday. If you don't know your birthday, change it when the chirping starts. But change it.

Fire moves faster than you think. It doubles in size every thirty seconds. This is not a statistic; it is the speed of a room becoming somewhere you cannot be.

Know two ways out of every room. If one is a window, know how it opens. If it's painted shut, it is not an exit. Fix it or choose another.

If you cannot leave, get to the room you chose in Section IV. Close the door. Put wet towels at the gap. Open the window. Hang something bright from it. Stay low. The air near the floor is cooler and clearer. You can survive smoke you cannot see through, but only if you stay beneath it.

## XI. The Empty Room

There will be a room you don't use. Maybe it's small. Maybe it's awkward. Maybe it's the one that gets too hot in summer and too cold in winter, the one with the outlet that doesn't work, the one you keep meaning to do something with.

This room is not wasted space. It is reserves. It is the room that can become anything. Storage. Quarantine. The room where someone can sleep who didn't expect to stay. The room where you can close the door and hear the silence and know that on the other side of that door, everything is still there.

Don't fill this room on purpose. Let it stay empty until it tells you what it's for. It will.

## XII. After

After the pipe bursts and you find the shutoff and clean the floor and stand in the kitchen holding a towel wondering why you're shaking—

After the power fails and the silence is so complete you can hear the refrigerator stop humming and realize that this is what the building sounds like without electricity, which is what it sounded like before anyone lived here at all—

After you stand at the top of the stairs at two in the morning listening to a sound that might be the wind, might be the neighbor, might be nothing—

After the thing you were worried about doesn't happen, and you're left standing in the building with all your preparations and none of the emergencies—

Sit down. You have defended this building. Not from anything in particular. From everything in general. From the specific gravity of caring about a place, which is the act of believing it will still be standing when you return.

Lock the door you use to take out the trash. You'll remember this time. But the lock doesn't rely on you remembering. That's what it's for.
