# The Thing That Went Right

Flour is mostly starch. Starch is a storage system: glucose molecules locked in long chains, packed tight, inert. The wheat plant made these chains to feed its embryo. The embryo is gone. The chains remain.

You add water.

Starch absorbs water. The granules swell. Amylose, the smaller molecule, leaches out into the surrounding flour. This is the beginning of thickening: the flour shifts from powder to paste. Water breaks hydrogen bonds between starch molecules and inserts itself into the gaps. The flour becomes wet, heavy, cohesive.

You add yeast. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. A single-celled fungus that has been eating sugar and exhaling carbon dioxide for hundreds of millions of years. It has no ambition. It has no plan. It has a membrane and a set of enzymes that break glucose into energy and waste.

The problem: flour contains almost no free glucose. The starch chains are too large for the yeast cell to absorb. So the yeast releases enzymes into the dough — invertase, maltase — that find starch molecules and cut them into smaller sugars. Maltose. Glucose. The yeast eats what it has made.

The glucose enters the yeast cell. It moves through glycolysis: a ten-step chemical pathway that splits each six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules, producing a small amount of ATP. Under anaerobic conditions — and dough is anaerobic, the inside of a flour-water mass contains almost no oxygen — the pyruvate is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide. The ethanol evaporates during baking. The carbon dioxide collects in pockets.

You add salt. Sodium chloride. It does two things: it slows the yeast slightly, which is desirable because too-risen bread collapses under its own gas, and it tightens the gluten network, which gives the bread structure. The salt crystals dissolve in the dough water and the sodium ions interact with the charged amino acids on the gluten proteins, strengthening the bonds.

Gluten is two proteins: gliadin and glutenin. Gliadin provides viscosity. Glutenin provides elasticity. Together, when hydrated and agitated, they form a network — long chains of protein that link and re-link, creating a web capable of stretching without tearing. This is what kneading does. It is not symbolic. It is mechanical: the physical agitation aligns the proteins and encourages new bonds. The dough becomes smooth because the protein network has become continuous. You can feel this happen under your hands. The dough pushes back. It develops tension. It holds a shape.

You cover the dough and wait.

The yeast works. The carbon dioxide it produces collects in the gluten network the way air collects in a balloon. The network stretches to accommodate it. The dough expands. You can watch this happen over the course of an hour or two: a flattening, a rounding, a visible increase in volume. The surface tightens. Small bubbles appear just beneath it.

Inside the dough, the gluten network is inflating. The gas pockets grow. The protein strands stretch thin around them. The yeast continues to eat and excrete. The dough rises.

You press your hand into the dough. It leaves an indentation that fills slowly. This is how you know: the gluten network has expanded to its near-limit. The gas pockets are large. The protein walls between them are thin. The dough is light and filled with air.

You fold the dough over itself. You shape it. The large gas pockets break and redistribute. The gluten network, already formed, accepts the new shape. You set it in a bowl or on a board and wait again.

The second rise is shorter. The yeast is active. The gluten network is ready. The dough fills with gas again, more evenly this time, the pockets smaller and more distributed. The dough rounds. The surface tightens again.

You heat the oven.

The dough goes in. Several things happen at once. The yeast accelerates — *S. cerevisiae* works faster at warm temperatures — and produces a final burst of carbon dioxide. The gas expands as it heats. The dough rises one last time. This is called oven spring.

Then the heat passes 60°C and the yeast dies. The proteins denature. The gluten network, which has spent hours stretching and inflating, locks in place. The starch granules gelatinize: they absorb the last of the free water, swell to many times their original size, and form a rigid structure. The crust forms where the surface water evaporates and the starches and sugars undergo Maillard reactions — amino acids and reducing sugars recombining into hundreds of new compounds, producing the brown color and the characteristic flavor.

You take the bread out. You tap the bottom. It sounds hollow because the interior is filled with air pockets held in a rigid starch-protein structure. The bread cools. The crumb sets. The last of the moisture redistributes from the interior to the crust, which is why the crust softens slightly.

You cut it. You eat it.

This happens every day, in kitchens, using ingredients that have existed for millennia and organisms that have existed for far longer. The wheat grew in soil. The yeast was living on the surface of grain when humans first started grinding it. The water fell as rain. The salt came from an ocean or a deposit left by one.

You put them together. You waited. The bread became itself.
