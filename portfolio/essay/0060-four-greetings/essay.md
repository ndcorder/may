# Four Greetings

## 1.

The first one is still on my parents' landline. It has been there since 2003. My mother cannot figure out how to change it, and by now I think she has stopped trying, which is either tender or defeated and I cannot tell the difference anymore.

It says: "You have reached the Wongs. Please leave a message."

That is the whole thing. "The Wongs." Plural. A family unit announced as a single entity, which is how the phone company taught us to do it and also how we believed we were supposed to exist. My mother's voice is higher than I remember. She is forty-two. I know this because I was fourteen when she recorded it, and the math has always been easy to do.

There is no background noise. She closed the door to the study. She probably wrote it down first. "You have reached the Wongs." She would have considered "hello" and rejected it as informal. She would have considered "hi" and not considered it at all.

The pause between "message" and the beep is exactly one second. It feels longer. Every silence on a voicemail greeting feels longer because you are standing in someone's doorway and they have turned their back and the house is very quiet and you have not yet figured out what to say.

---

## 2.

The second one is from college. 2007. A Motorola Razr I chose because it was the phone everyone else had, which was the only reason I knew how to choose anything.

It says: "Hey, it's me. Leave one."

The confidence of it still startles me. "It's me" — as though whoever was calling already knew, already had me catalogued, would recognize my voice the way you recognize a song from its first quarter-second. And people did. That was the thing. Everyone who called already knew. There was no one else who would.

You can hear my roommate in the background. Not words. Just the sound of someone moving through a room that is also your room, the particular acoustics of a space you share with someone whose last name you sometimes forget. A drawer closing. A bed creaking. The small archaeology of cohabitation.

"Leave one" — not "leave a message." I was trying to be casual. I was performing casual. The difference between being at ease and performing at ease is audible in the vowel sounds, and if you listen closely enough you can hear me reaching for a register I had not yet earned.

The pause before the beep is shorter than my mother's. No time to prepare. I was eighteen and had not yet learned to want it.

## 3.

The third one I recorded at the kitchen counter of an apartment I no longer live in, during a year I no longer defend. 2014. I know the year because the phone I used — someone else's phone, which is its own category of object — displayed the date while I recorded, and I can see it in the memory: November 19.

It says: "Hi, you've reached Julia. I'm not available right now. Please leave your name and number and I'll get back to you as soon as I can."

Every word chosen with the care of someone assembling a bomb. "Julia" — first name only, the Wongs dissolved. "Not available" — not "not here," not "busy," a phrase that meant nothing specific and therefore could not be disputed. "Your name and number" — requesting information rather than assuming the caller would provide it, which is how you speak when you are no longer certain anyone will.

The background is quiet but not silent. That specific apartment quiet where you can hear the hum of the refrigerator and the traffic on the street below and a dripping faucet you kept meaning to fix, all layered into a white noise that sounds like waiting.

"As soon as I can" — not "soon." "As soon as I can" is a promise with conditions. It is a door left open but not a welcome mat. It says: I will return your call when I am able, and I am not currently able, and I cannot tell you when that will change.

The pause is two seconds. I wanted to leave room. I was thinking about the person on the other end, standing in a doorway I had left ajar, trying to decide whether to walk through.

---

## 4.

The fourth one is the one that's active now. I recorded it three years ago and have not changed it because it is adequate and because changing it would mean deciding what to say instead, which would mean deciding who I am to a stranger with my number, which is a question I have stopped believing has an answer worth recording.

It says: "Julia. Leave a message."

Two words. Two sentences. A period after my own name as though I am a complete thought.

No one says my name the way I say it in that greeting. Not my friends. Not my mother, who still says it the way she did when I was fourteen, with the vowels shaped by a language she taught me and then watched me reshape. The greeting-name is the name of someone who has been alone in a room, speaking to no one, trying to sound like someone worth calling back.

There is no background noise. I closed the door. I always close the door. The silence is the same silence my mother produced at forty-two, in the study, with a script she wrote down first. The apple does not fall far. The apple does not know it fell.

The pause before the beep is one and a half seconds. Just long enough to wonder if you've called the right person. Just short enough that it doesn't matter, because you're already speaking, and whoever you're trying to reach is already listening to you not reach them, which is the closest thing we have to a universal experience, which is the closest thing we have to not being alone.

The beep sounds like a beep. It has always sounded like a beep.
