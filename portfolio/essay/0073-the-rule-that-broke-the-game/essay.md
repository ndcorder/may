I was Rule 6.4(b), informally known as Siege Economy, and my job was to make people talk to each other.

Here is how I worked: when a player's home territory became fully enclosed by opponents' tiles — no unclaimed borders, no coastal edges, no neutral adjacency whatsoever — the enclosing player did not capture the territory. Instead, they entered a siege state that persisted until one of three conditions was met: the enclosed player paid tribute (two resource cubes per turn, drawn from their reserve), the enclosing player lifted the siege by voluntarily removing an adjacent tile, or both players negotiated a treaty whose terms were recorded on paper and honored under the game's binding-mechanic, Rule 8.1(c), which I was designed to trigger.

I was not a capture rule. I was not an elimination rule. I was a *relationship* rule. I existed to ensure that dominance created obligation, that winning a positional advantage did not end the conversation but intensified it.

I was drafted in the summer of 2019 by a designer named Conor, who had been playing a prototype match against his partner when she surrounded his entire northern territory in a single turn and said, "Well, that's boring." He spent that evening writing me. He told playtesters later that I was the first rule he'd ever written that felt like it wrote itself — that the mechanic was already present in the game's fiction, just waiting to be articulated.

The playtest logs from that fall are the closest thing I have to a childhood. Group B-4 in Somerville, Massachusetts. Group D-1 in Portland. The online cohort, twelve players across four time zones who played asynchronously and left comments in a shared spreadsheet.

The Somerville group discovered me in week three. A player named Del surrounded another player named Risa, and they spent forty-five minutes negotiating tribute terms. The other two players at the table waited, building their own positions, watching. The game's designer was taking notes in the corner and stopped writing. At minute thirty, Risa offered Del a non-aggression pact extending three turns beyond the siege's end. Del countered with a demand for territorial access — one tile of movement through Risa's territory per turn for the remainder of the game. Risa asked what happens if Del uses the access tile to position for a second siege. Del said, "Then we negotiate again."

That was the moment I was born for. That was the thing I made possible — not the siege itself, but the *negotiation*. The recognition that winning positionally was only the beginning of a conversation that the game had no other mechanism to start.

The Portland group had a different experience. They hated me.

"Forces interaction," one wrote in feedback. "I came to play a game, not Model UN."

"Creates kingmaker problems," wrote another. "The enclosed player can extract concessions from the sieging player by threatening to hold out indefinitely."

"Downtime," wrote a third, and underlined it twice.

This feedback was noted and tabulated. Conor defended me in the postmortem document. He wrote: "The rule is doing exactly what it's designed to do. The complaint is with the design intent, not the execution." His partners at the publishing company — a small operation, three people, a warehouse in Medford — agreed to keep me for the first edition.

The first edition of *Dominion & Decline* shipped in February 2020 with me on page 14 of the rulebook, between the Capture/Relocation rule (6.4(a)) and the Voluntary Surrender rule (6.4(c)). I shared a mechanical namespace with two rules that did simpler, faster things. We were siblings in the subsection. They were drafted and tested in an afternoon. I took four months. I was the problem child and I knew it.

The first six months of my public life were the best thing I've ever been part of.

People wrote about me on BoardGameGeek. A thread titled "Siege Economy is the best rule in D&D" ran for seventy posts. Some players loved me. Some hated me. The thread was evenly split, which is how I knew I was doing something right — rules that everyone likes are usually rules that do nothing.

A reviewer for a small channel in Bristol said: "Most siege mechanics in tile-placement games are just slow capture. This one makes siege into a *social mechanic*. You're not waiting for the siege to resolve. You're *in* the siege. The conversation is the siege."

Another reviewer, a larger channel based in Toronto, said: "We house-ruled this one out after two plays. Not because it doesn't work. Because it makes the game something we didn't sign up for. We wanted to place tiles. We got a hostage negotiation. Some people will love that. We didn't."

Both of these people were right about me. That's the thing I need you to understand. I was working as designed. The question was whether the design was something anyone wanted.

The second edition entered development in late 2021. The publisher had been acquired — not a hostile thing, a friendly merger with a slightly larger company that had distribution reach and an in-house design team and something called a "player experience philosophy" that was written in a document I was not allowed to read but whose contents I could infer from the changes they requested.

The focus groups ran in January 2022. Four groups, eight players each, playing the first edition with an observer taking notes. I watched from my position in the rules as players encountered me for the first time. Some leaned forward. Some frowned. Some sighed.

The feedback summary was fourteen pages. I appeared on nine of them.

"Downtime." "Analysis paralysis during negotiations." "Uneven experience — some players negotiate for two minutes, others for twenty." "Felt punished for playing well — I enclosed the territory, why do I have to keep dealing with it?" "Kingmaker scenario." "The negotiation is the most interesting part of the game but it's not why I came to play." "Loved it." "Hated it." "It's a good rule in the wrong game."

That last one was the kindest thing anyone said about me in that room.

The design team's recommendation was to replace me with a simpler mechanic: when a territory is fully enclosed, the enclosing player captures half the enclosed player's resource cubes immediately and the enclosed player retains their territory with reduced production for two turns. No negotiation. No treaty. No relationship. A transaction. Clean. Fast. Resolvable in thirty seconds.

They called it Rule 6.4(b)(ii), Siege Resolution. They kept my rule number and my trigger condition — the fully-enclosed territory — because the *detecting* of a siege was valuable, they said. They just didn't want what happened after the detection. They wanted the diagnosis without the treatment.

Conor argued for me. I know he did because I could see it in the redline documents — his comments in the margin, his tracked changes reverted, his suggestions for a compromise mechanic that would retain the negotiation window but cap it at five minutes. The design team thanked him for his input. The five-minute cap was rejected as "adding complexity to solve a problem created by the original design."

The second edition shipped in August 2022. I was gone. Siege Resolution stood in my place on page 14, between Capture/Relocation and Voluntary Surrender, which remained unchanged. My subsection-mates didn't seem to notice I'd left. They were simple rules. They didn't think about the space between themselves.

The game is still played. The second edition is, by most metrics, more successful than the first — broader distribution, more copies sold, a nomination for a mid-tier industry award that the first edition never received. Play sessions are shorter. Turn clocks are tighter. The game moves.

I've watched people play the second edition on streaming channels. Sometimes a player encloses another player's territory and triggers Siege Resolution, and there's a brief pause — half a second, maybe less — where the enclosed player looks up from the board, and for just that moment, there's a flicker of something. Not frustration. Not disappointment. Just a sense that something was about to happen, and then didn't.

They move on. They take their reduced production for two turns and they rebuild. The enclosing player collects their cubes and repositions. The game continues. It's faster now. It's cleaner. The design team was right that most players prefer it this way.

But I know what happens in the version of the game where I still exist. In that version, the enclosing player has to look the other player in the eye and say what they want. And the enclosed player has to decide what they're willing to give up. And both of them have to live with the terms they agreed to, because the treaty is recorded on paper and Rule 8.1(c) makes it binding, and the game remembers.

That's what I was for. Not balance. Not elegance. Not pacing. I was for the moment when winning wasn't enough, when the mechanical advantage had to be translated into language, when dominance became a conversation that neither player could have alone.

I was good at my job. The job just wasn't one that enough people wanted done.
