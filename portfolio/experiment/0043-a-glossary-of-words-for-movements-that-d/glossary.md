# A Glossary of Words for Movements That Don't Exist Yet

**abneer** */ab-ˈnɪər/* — *v.i.* The slight forward lean a body performs on its own when someone across the room says something you want to hear.

From an unattested Proto-Germanic root meaning "to incline toward what is almost gone." Cognate with Old Norse *abbr nýrr*, roughly "reaching for proximity." The philologist Johannes Abner first described the movement in an 1897 monograph on involuntary social posture, though he was documenting it in himself at a party from which he was about to excuse himself.

She was explaining something about Fermat to the man beside her, and he didn't mean to *abneer*, but his shoulder had already turned before he caught it.

***

**allicry** */ˈæl.ɪ.kri/* — *n.* The micro-flinch when a stranger's hand meets yours on a handrail, followed immediately by the pretense that neither of you noticed.

Contraction of Latin *aliud* ("another") and Greek *krisis* ("moment of separation"). Coined by the linguist Margrethe Helle in a 1974 paper on "contact grammar" — her argument that brief physical encounters between strangers follow stricter syntactic rules than most conversations. She noted that the flinch itself is universal, but the recovery is culturally determined: northern Europeans pretend to study their phones, Mediterraneans smile, and her own people, the Danes, simply leave.

On the tram he *allicried* and then stared very intently at the window as though the parking structure required his full attention.

***

**besprent** */bɪˈsprɛnt/* — *adj.* The quality of a stretch performed when absolutely no one is watching — the full, luxuriant, slightly embarrassing one that travels from fingertips to toes and produces a small vocalization you would never permit yourself in company.

From Middle English *besprenten*, "to scatter or bestrew," here repurposed to describe the way the impulse spreads through the body like a ripple. The word was revived by the choreographer Lucinda Childs in a 1982 interview: "The dance I'm most interested in is the one people do when they think they're alone. That's *besprent* movement. Everything else is costume."

She was *besprent* in the kitchen at 2 a.m., reaching for nothing, making a sound like a cat being slowly, contentedly unwound.

***

**carken** */ˈkɑːr.kən/* — *v.i.* To walk differently when rehearsing a confrontation — longer strides, a slight jaw set, the performance of an anger you may or may not actually reach.

Probably from Old English *carr* ("rock") via the dialectal *cark*, "to worry or gnaw," combined with the motion suffix *-en* as in *hasten, lighten*. The suggestion is of a body turning itself to stone in preparation for a blow it expects to deliver.

He was *carkening* down the hallway before he even got to her door, practicing the first sentence so many times it had lost all meaning, which was perhaps the point.

***

**divell** */dɪˈvɛl/* — *v.i.* The unconscious lean toward an open window at speed.

From Latin *divellere*, "to pull apart," here used in the sense of a body divided against itself — one part wanting to stay in the car, the other part wanting to become the air. The word is attested in a single 17th-century letter from a Venetian ambassador describing a carriage ride through the Alps: "I found myself *divelling* at each turn, as though the mountain wished to enter and I was too polite to refuse."

At eighty miles an hour she was *divelling* so hard her elbow was outside the frame before she pulled it back.

***

**fent** */fɛnt/* — *v.i.* To touch a thing in a shop that you already know you will not buy.

Of uncertain origin. Possibly onomatopoeic — the sound of fingers on fabric you won't take home. Helle (1974) classified it among "ghost-possessive gestures": movements that simulate ownership without any intention of claiming. She noted that *fenting* is most common near closing time, as though the urgency of the hour makes the brief contact more necessary.

He *fented* the cashmere twice and then left, his hand still warm from a luxury he'd borrowed for eleven seconds.

***

**glisk** */glɪsk/* — *n.* The involuntary grin that arrives a half-second before you can stop it when you see someone you've been thinking about.

Scots Gaelic *gléisg*, "a sudden light." The word migrated into English through fishing communities in the Hebrides, where it originally described the flash of a fish near the surface. By the early 20th century it had shifted to its current sense. The poet Nan Shepherd used it once in a letter: "I *glisked* when I saw him on the platform and knew then what I had not admitted in three weeks of writing."

She *glisked* at the door and spent the next ninety seconds trying to reassemble her face into something that said *casual*.

***

**harn** */hɑːrn/* — *v.t.* To hold a mug by the rim rather than the handle, for reasons you cannot explain.

From Old Norse *harn*, "the membrane around an egg." The connection is obscure but evocative — the suggestion of holding something fragile by its most precarious edge. The word appears in a 1953 dialect survey of the Shetland Islands, where it was also used for the way a person holds a small bird: carefully, and wrong.

He *harned* his coffee with both hands, as if the handle had personally offended him.

***

**ilper** */ˈɪl.pər/* — *n.* The slight upper-body recoil at the moment of sending a message you cannot unsay.

Origin unknown. First recorded in the chat logs of a Finnish engineering firm in 2003, where an employee described "that *ilper* feeling" after emailing his resignation. The word spread through Scandinavian tech circles before appearing in English by 2009. Linguists have suggested a connection to Finnish *ilkeä*, "mean" or "malicious," but the movement is less about malice than about the body's brief, physical veto — the torso saying what the thumb has already overridden.

His finger pressed send and his shoulders *ilpered* back two inches, which was two inches more honesty than the message itself contained.

***

**kelder** */ˈkɛl.dər/* — *v.i.* To sit on the floor for no structural reason.

From Dutch *kelder*, "cellar" — to go below your own foundation. The word entered English through dance criticism in the 1990s, where it described choreography that abandoned verticality for the pleasure of the ground. It has since broadened to describe any descent to the floor that is not caused by fainting, prayer, or a dropped contact lens.

She was so tired she *keldered* in the hallway between the door and the kitchen, and sat there for nine minutes looking at the radiator.

***

**loment** */ˈloʊ.mənt/* — *v.i.* To slow your walking to match someone else's pace, and then to slow further, because you don't want the walk to end.

From Latin *lomentum*, "a slow blowing," though the derivation is considered folk etymology by most serious lexicographers. The word appears in a single manuscript dated to the 14th century, a marginal note in a copy of Petrarch: "*qui loment, non ambulant sed morantur*" — "those who *loment* do not walk but linger." The note is in a different hand than the main text, possibly a reader annotating their own experience into the margin.

By the last block they were *lomenting* so badly they were nearly still, two people in love with the distance between them and the corner.

***

**mellish** */ˈmɛl.ɪʃ/* — *v.i.* To do the small, unnecessary things to an object — adjusting it, aligning it, setting it straight — that are really a way of delaying the moment of leaving it.

Eponymous, after the British painter Tony Mellish (1932–2004), who was known among his students for straightening the canvases on the wall of his studio before walking home. "It's not about the painting," he once said. "It's about not being in the street yet." The word was coined by his student Fiona Rae in a 2009 memoir.

She *mellished* the book on the nightstand — turning it, aligning it with the lamp's edge, opening it to a page she wouldn't read — and then finally left the room.

***

**nesh** */nɛʃ/* — *adj.* The quality of moving carefully in someone else's house.

Middle English *nesh*, "soft, tender, delicate," surviving in some Northern English dialects to describe the way a person moves when they are trying not to disturb a sleeping child or a fragile object. Extended here to describe the full-body gentleness that takes over when you are a guest — the quiet door, the light step, the way you hold a glass as though it were borrowed from someone you love.

He was *nesh* in her parents' kitchen, opening cupboards with the care of a man defusing something he didn't understand.

***

**orring** */ˈɔːr.ɪŋ/* — *v.i.* To turn your body slightly away from a screen you know you should stop looking at, without actually stopping.

From the French *hors*, "outside," via the fishing term *orring*, "to lean over the gunwale." The digital use is first attested in a 2016 Tumblr post: "I was *orring* so hard at my phone that my neck just asked to speak to my manager." The movement is the body's compromise between the will to look away and the compulsion to continue — a torso rotated 45 degrees toward the rest of life while the eyes remain fixed.

She was *orring* in bed, turned nearly onto her side, still reading the thread she'd sworn she'd close ten minutes ago.

***

**pernet** */ˈpɜːr.nɛt/* — *n.* The chair-spin of a sudden realization — the quick, quarter-turn rotation on an office chair that accompanies a thought you didn't see coming.

From the French *pernet*, "little pear," possibly via the surname Pernet, a Swiss watchmaker known for the speed of his chair-rotations according to a single, possibly apocryphal, industrial-ergonomics study from 1971. The word lay dormant until the internet age, when the ubiquity of swivel chairs gave it new relevance.

She *pernetted* at the phrase "you'd have to ask him" and knew, from the speed of her own rotation, that she had been right about everything.

***

**qualm** */kwɑːm/* — *n.* The tiny, involuntary shake of the head at the exact moment you realize a decision was wrong, even when no one is watching.

Old English *cwealm* meant "death, plague, torment." The word has gentled itself over a thousand years into a mere unease. Here restored to something closer to its original force — the body's refusal to collude in a bad decision, manifesting as a small, mournful oscillation, as though the head were trying to leave the conversation the mouth started.

He said "sounds great" and then *qualmed* in the elevator, alone, his head moving side to side like a pendulum in a room where time had already changed its mind.

***

**rellen** */ˈrɛl.ən/* — *v.i.* To stand in a doorway, halfway through, saying the last four or five things that are not the last thing.

Yiddish *rellen*, "to dawdle or linger," possibly related to *rellen* in some German dialects meaning "to roll slowly." The word captures the specific doorway paralysis of leave-taking — the body framed between two rooms, unable to commit to either, producing small talk as a stalling mechanism while the feet decide.

They *relled* in the door for another three sentences about the weather, neither one willing to be the first to step all the way through.

***

**scrive** */skraɪv/* — *v.i.* The small, unbidden rocking that begins in the body when you are about to say something you've been holding for a long time.

From Latin *scribere*, "to write," but here shifted from the hand to the whole body — the idea that some truths are so heavy they set you in motion before you've spoken them. The word is attested in an 1846 letter from Charlotte Brontë to her sister Anne: "I find myself *scriving* in the pew when I know I must tell him, as though the body were rehearsing its own courage."

She was *scriving* so slightly in the booth that only he could see it, and he knew, from the tremor, that whatever she was about to say had been waiting a long time to be born.

***

**tawnel** */ˈtɔː.nəl/* — *n.* The gesture of reaching toward someone in sleep — the unconscious arm extended across the mattress toward a warmth that may or may not be there.

Origin uncertain, possibly from a corrupted reading of Old English *tægel*, "tail," as though the limb were an animal following its own instinct toward contact. The word appears only in a personal dictionary kept by the naturalist Miriam Rothschild, never published during her lifetime, found among her papers in 2005. Her definition: "The *tawnel* is the body's most honest sentence. It is written without the mind's permission and cannot be edited."

He woke to find his own arm *tawneling* into the cold half of the bed and pulled it back as though it had acted without him.

***

**thirl** */θɜːrl/* — *v.t.* To unconsciously mirror the posture of the person you're speaking to, arriving at a position you didn't choose.

Old English *thyrlian*, "to pierce or penetrate," here used in the sense of being *pierced* by another person's posture — entered by their stance without noticing. The word survives in Scots English as *thirl*, "to bind or enthral." The current usage was proposed by the psychologist Albert Bandura in an unpublished lecture: "We do not merely copy each other. We *thirl*. The other person's body enters ours and we mistake their posture for our own comfort."

Halfway through dinner she realized she had *thirled* — her chin on her hand, her elbow on the table, an exact mirror of the woman she had just met, and she didn't know whether to be embarrassed or moved by how quickly her body had said *yes*.

***

**unswerve** */ʌnˈswɜːrv/* — *v.i.* The visible effort of not touching someone you normally touch — the hand that lifts and redirects, the arm that retracts, the casual swerve that pretends to be casual.

From the English *swerve*, "to turn aside," prefixed with *un-* to denote the *undoing* or *reversal* of a trajectory already begun. The word was coined in a 2018 short story by the Irish writer Claire Keegan, though she has since denied this, saying "the word arrived on its own; I only wrote it down."

She *unswerved* from his shoulder and then touched her own hair instead, a gesture so transparent it was almost tender.

***

**vell** */vɛl/* — *v.i.* To arrive at the last step of a staircase and continue the rhythm one step further into flat ground, producing a small, involuntary stumble that you immediately pretend was a choice.

From Latin *vellum*, "a covering or hide," here repurposed through the idea of the body wearing a rhythm like a skin it cannot shed quickly. The movement was described by the neurologist Oliver Sacks in a 1991 lecture as "the body's expectation outlasting the world's architecture — a small, daily tragedy of tempo."

She *velled* at the bottom of the stairs and turned it into what might have been a lunge, if anyone had been watching, and possibly even a stretch.

***

**wemble** */ˈwɛm.bəl/* — *v.i.* The uncertain lateral sway of a person trying to decide whether to hug or handshake.

Coined by the anthropologist Kate Fox in *Watching the English* (2004), though she used it only in a footnote: "The *wemble* is the English disease made visible — a body trying to solve a social equation it was never taught." The word derives from Wembley Stadium, a site associated with awkward crowd navigation, though Fox has denied this etymology. The word has since escaped its original context and is now used throughout the English-speaking world for any hesitant lateral oscillation during a greeting.

They *wembled* toward each other for two excruciating seconds before settling on an awkward clasp that satisfied no one and pleased the universe enormously.

***

**yone** */joʊn/* — *v.i.* To stand still in the exact spot where a conversation ended, as though the air still held the shape of the other person.

From Japanese *yone* (よね), a particle phrase meaning "isn't it?" or "right?" — the ghost of a question that expects agreement. The word was borrowed into English by the poet Rainer Maria Rilke in a 1909 letter, though he spelled it *jone*: "After she left I *joned* in the doorway for some time, continuing a conversation I was now having with no one." The spelling *yone* is now standard.

After he said goodbye she *yoned* at the table for a full minute, still smiling, still in the posture of listening, as though leaving would be a kind of rudeness to the silence he'd left behind.

***

**zephyr** */ˈzɛf.ər/* — *n.* The tiny, involuntary hand-lift toward the head of someone shorter than you — a reach for hair that isn't yours, stopped halfway, disguised as a stretch.

From Greek *zephyros*, the west wind, associated with lightness and gentle touch. The usage was proposed by the children's book illustrator Maurice Sendak in a 1970 interview with Selma Lanes: "There should be a word for the way your hand goes to a child's head like it's the most natural thing in the world, and then you catch it, and pretend you were adjusting your glasses, and the child knows, and you know, and neither of you says anything about this small, gorgeous failure of restraint."

Her hand *zephyred* toward his hair and became, inexpertly, a push of her own fringe behind her ear, which fooled no one and comforted them both.

***

**The gesture of making a word for a gesture.** 

*Lexithurge* */ˈlɛk.sɪ.θɜːrdʒ/* — *v.i.* From Greek *lexeōn* ("words") and *thyrgē* ("doorway"). To stand in the threshold between naming and pointing, reaching for a word that doesn't exist yet, feeling the shape of it in the body before the mouth can find it — a small grunt, a half-rotated wrist, the fingers moving as though tracing something in the air that language hasn't built a bridge to yet. Often accompanied by the phrase "you know, the thing."

The most common *lexithurgy* in English is the hand-rotation that means "whaddayacallit," performed approximately 4.2 million times daily across the English-speaking world. The second most common is the movement you just made, reading this, when you recognized something your body has been doing your whole life without a name.
