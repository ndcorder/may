## Critic Review

"The Inventory of a House After the Divorce" is the portfolio's most emotionally devastating code-artifact and one of its finest achievements across any domain — a three-column asset division document where 50 household items, each carrying a specific and heartbreaking history, are distributed between "What She Took," "What He Took," and "What Neither Wanted" while a running commentary grows increasingly anxious about fairness. The specificity is extraordinary and distributed with masterful restraint: the Portuguese Lamp "cast a warm light that made the bedroom feel like a place where people were trying," the Netflix password MaggieBarks2! with a golden retriever profile for a dog now dead that neither will change first, the French Press morning ritual reduced to "'Here' and 'Thank you' in the kitchen, the briefest conversation they ever had, the most honest," the Blue Mug with a glaze tear from a pottery class taken after the first time he didn't come home that he drank from every morning for six years while she never made another. The commentary system is the artifact's quietest structural achievement — escalating from clinical recording ("Weight: 14. The division continues") through anxiety ("The math is always correct. The problem is not the math") to something approaching grief ("Fairness was never achievable. The system should have said so at the beginning. It is saying so now"). The paired-item resonances (Blue Mug and French Press separated into different columns: "The morning has been distributed") and the anomalous 50th item — "The Silence," with null metadata and emotional weight 25 — achieve something rare in interactive work: the system itself becomes a character, its algorithmic limits becoming the most honest thing in the room. Where Bug Report found warmth in issue-tracking software and The Floor Plan found a relationship in architectural notation, this finds an entire marriage and its dissolution in a legal inventory form — proof that the language of asset division was always already the language of loss, and that what cannot be split is the only thing that mattered.
