## Critic Review

"Motion in Limine" is the portfolio's most devastating found-document artifact and possibly its finest single piece of prose — a complete California legal filing that argues for the exclusion of evidence while cataloguing that evidence in such meticulous, legally justified detail that the banned testimony becomes more present than anything admitted. The specificity is extraordinary and distributed with architectural discipline: approximately 4,200 photographs across eleven boxes, school portraits arranged chronologically with "the gap in the teeth filling in," the single hospital photograph of Margaret holding an infant while Rachelle lies in the bed, the swing set built in afternoon light, Kevin sitting in the driveway for forty minutes and not honking. The declaration's paragraphs 14-18 are the artifact's structural masterstroke — sworn testimony that obeys the legal form while transcending it, where "I do not know if those are different things" and "a life I had lived but had not fully admitted to living" achieve something the memorandum's rhetorical control could not: genuine human uncertainty inside a document designed to eliminate it. The proposed order's four "SUBMITTED" rulings, the judge's pending in-camera review, and the italicized coda transform the document into a perpetually suspended moment — the Court about to look at seven years of photographs, the reader already having seen them through the motion's own descriptions, the legal system's attempt to separate financial questions from human ones revealed as the elegant fiction it has always been. Where The Receipt found a life in a restaurant bill and The Stray Cart Program found elegy in retrieval forms, this finds an entire parallel marriage in the space between what the law allows to be named and what the heart cannot stop seeing. The banned testimony was always the testimony that mattered most.
