# MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case involves the dissolution of a twenty-two-year marriage. The parties were married on September 6, 2001, and separated on August 12, 2023. The issues properly before this Court concern the identification, valuation, and division of community property assets and the determination of spousal support obligations. These are financial questions that require a dispassionate, evidence-based analysis.

Defendant, however, appears determined to transform these proceedings into a referendum on the personal choices made during the course of the marriage — choices that, while perhaps of great emotional significance to the parties, are legally irrelevant to the property division issues before this Court. Defendant's counsel has indicated an intention to introduce evidence of Plaintiff's separate property acquisitions, personal relationships, and private activities that have no bearing on the legal questions at issue. Such evidence serves only one purpose: to provoke an emotional response from the Court that will cloud its judgment on the financial matters properly before it.

The evidence Defendant seeks to introduce is, in the main, a chronicle of a separate life lived alongside the marriage. It consists of photographs, financial documents, and testimony concerning relationships, properties, and activities that Plaintiff maintained outside the marital home. The prejudice inherent in such evidence is self-evident. It invites the Court to punish one party for the perceived moral failings of maintaining a separate existence — a separate life — while remaining legally married. California law does not permit this.

This motion seeks to ensure that the trial remains focused on what the law requires: a fair and equitable division of the community estate.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The parties were married for approximately twenty-two years. Two children were born of the marriage: Lily, born 2004, and Marcus, born 2007. Both children are now adults. Throughout the marriage, Plaintiff was employed as a project manager at various technology firms. Defendant worked as a certified public accountant and, in 2012, became a partner at the firm of Dewey, Liu & Associates.

The marriage experienced periods of strain. The parties attended marriage counseling in 2009, 2014, and again in 2019. Throughout this period, Plaintiff maintained the marital home in San Marino and was the primary caregiver for the children.

In 2018, Plaintiff purchased the Altadena Property without informing Defendant. The property was purchased using funds from the Account, which Plaintiff had maintained separately since approximately 2015. The Altadena Property is titled solely in Plaintiff's name. Plaintiff does not dispute that the source of the funds used to purchase the Altadena Property may, in part, constitute community property earnings. The characterization of the Altadena Property — as community or separate — is a legal question properly before this Court. What is not properly before this Court is the wealth of personal, intimate, and emotionally charged evidence Defendant proposes to introduce in connection with that characterization.

In the course of discovery, Defendant obtained possession of eleven boxes of photographic materials from the marital home. These boxes contain photographs spanning approximately eight years. They depict, among other things: Plaintiff's personal relationships outside the marriage; social gatherings, holiday celebrations, and travel undertaken with individuals other than Defendant; and the daily life, activities, and milestones of a family unit separate from the one maintained in the marital home. Plaintiff does not dispute the existence of this separate life. Plaintiff does not dispute that the photographs exist. Plaintiff objects to their introduction at trial on the grounds that they are irrelevant to the property division issues, are inadmissible character evidence, and are substantially more prejudicial than probative.

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Altadena Property Evidence Is Irrelevant to Property Characterization

Evidence Code § 350 provides that no evidence is admissible except relevant evidence. Relevant evidence is defined by Evidence Code § 210 as evidence "having any tendency in reason to prove or disprove any disputed fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action."

The characterization of the Altadena Property as community or separate property turns on the source of the funds used to acquire it and the intentions of the parties with respect to its ownership. *In re Marriage of Weaver* (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 858. These are questions that can be resolved through bank records, title documents, and testimony concerning the movement of funds. They do not require — and will not be assisted by — evidence concerning the circumstances under which Plaintiff acquired the property, the reasons for keeping the purchase undisclosed, or the personal uses to which the property was put.

Defendant's counsel has indicated an intention to introduce testimony regarding the circumstances of the purchase — specifically, that Plaintiff acquired the property "secretly," that Defendant learned of the purchase only upon reviewing financial disclosures during dissolution proceedings, and that Plaintiff used the property as a residence for persons with whom Plaintiff had personal relationships. None of this evidence bears on the legal question of whether the funds used were community or separate. None of it is relevant.

The fact that Plaintiff did not inform Defendant of the purchase may be relevant to a claim of breach of fiduciary duty under Family Code § 721, but no such claim has been pleaded in this action. The fact that Plaintiff used the property as a residence for other persons is immaterial to its characterization as community or separate property. The fact that Plaintiff maintained a separate life — a life of which Defendant was unaware — is not a fact of consequence to any issue properly before this Court.

### B. Evidence of Plaintiff's Personal Relationships Is Inadmissible Character Evidence

Evidence Code § 1101(a) provides that "evidence of a person's character or a trait of his or her character... is inadmissible when offered to prove his or her conduct on a specified occasion."

Defendant seeks to introduce evidence of Plaintiff's relationship with Rachelle Tran, with whom Plaintiff was involved from approximately 2015 to 2023, and with other individuals whose names have been disclosed in discovery. The purpose of this evidence, as stated by Defendant's counsel at the case management conference on October 3, 2024, is to demonstrate Plaintiff's "pattern of concealment" and "deceptive character." This is precisely the use of character evidence prohibited by Evidence Code § 1101.

That Plaintiff maintained a romantic relationship with Ms. Tran for a period of approximately eight years is not in dispute. That Plaintiff traveled with Ms. Tran, purchased property in proximity to Ms. Tran's residence, and participated in social and family events with Ms. Tran and Ms. Tran's children is not in dispute. What Defendant seeks to do with this evidence — to demonstrate that Plaintiff is the kind of person who conceals, who deceives, who maintains a secret life — is to ask the Court to infer that because Plaintiff concealed one thing, Plaintiff must have concealed other things relevant to the property division.

This is character evidence offered to prove conduct. It is inadmissible.

### C. The Photographs and Documentary Evidence Are Substantially More Prejudicial Than Probative

Evidence Code § 352 grants this Court broad discretion to exclude evidence where its probative value is substantially outweighed by the probability that its admission will "create substantial danger of undue prejudice" or "confuse the issues."

The eleven boxes of photographic materials obtained by Defendant from the marital home contain approximately 4,200 photographs spanning the years 2015 through 2023. Plaintiff has reviewed these materials in preparation for this motion and can attest to their contents. They include, without limitation:

— Photographs of Plaintiff and Rachelle Tran at various restaurants, parks, and social occasions throughout the Los Angeles metropolitan area. Many of these photographs are informal in nature, apparently taken by the subjects themselves. In several, the parties are embracing.

— Photographs of holiday celebrations, including what appear to be Thanksgiving dinners, Christmas mornings, and birthday parties held at the Altadena Property. These photographs include, in addition to Plaintiff and Ms. Tran, individuals who appear to be Ms. Tran's extended family members and minor children. The children are depicted opening gifts, blowing out candles on cakes, and playing in the yard of the Altadena Property. The photographs depict, in total, approximately seven years of holiday celebrations — a visual record of a parallel domestic life.

— Photographs of a trip to Oahu, Hawaii in December 2019, including photographs of Plaintiff and Ms. Tran at Diamond Head, at the Polynesian Cultural Center, and on the beach at Lanikai. There are also photographs of a hotel room at the Halekulani Hotel, including a photograph of a bottle of champagne and two glasses on a nightstand, with the ocean visible through the window.

— Photographs of home improvement projects at the Altadena Property, including what appears to be the renovation of a kitchen in 2019 and the construction of a backyard play structure in 2020. The play structure photographs are particularly detailed, depicting Plaintiff assembling a wooden swing set with the assistance of two small children.

— Photographs of what appear to be the first days of school, Halloween costumes, soccer games, piano recitals, and swim meets featuring children to whom Plaintiff has no legal relationship but who appear in the photographs year after year, alongside Plaintiff, in the manner of family documentation.

— School photographs. Individual portraits, the kind taken each fall with a generic blue-gray background, arranged chronologically from 2016 to 2023. Two children. Year after year. The gap in the teeth filling in. The faces lengthening. Plaintiff has no legal relationship to these children, but there they are, in their school pictures, in a box in the marital home.

— A single photograph, undated but appearing to be from 2017 or 2018, of Plaintiff holding an infant in a hospital room. Ms. Tran is visible in the hospital bed. The infant is wrapped in a white blanket with blue and pink stripes. Plaintiff is looking down at the infant. Plaintiff is smiling.

The probative value of these photographs is nil. They do not establish the source of funds used to purchase the Altadena Property. They do not establish the amount of community funds deposited in or withdrawn from the Account. They do not resolve any contested factual issue relevant to the property division.

Their prejudice is immense.

These photographs will invite the Court to view Plaintiff not as a party to a property division proceeding, but as a figure in a domestic drama — a person who maintained two households, two lives, two families, for the better part of a decade. The Court will be asked to look at seven years of birthday parties, seven years of school photographs, seven years of a life conducted in parallel, and to form a moral judgment. This is precisely the danger Evidence Code § 352 is designed to prevent.

The law does not require the Court to look at these photographs. The law does not require the Court to see the years.

### D. The Bank Account Evidence Is Admissible Only as to Amounts, Not as to Purpose

Plaintiff does not object to the admission of bank statements from the Account for the purpose of tracing community funds. To the extent Defendant seeks to introduce evidence of specific withdrawals, transfers, or expenditures from the Account — including payments for the Altadena Property mortgage, home improvements, travel, gifts, or other expenses — such evidence is objectionable on relevance grounds. The source of the funds is relevant to characterization. The use of the funds is not.

Defendant's counsel may argue that the use of the funds is relevant to demonstrating that community funds were spent on non-community purposes. This is true only in the most general sense. The amounts are relevant. The specific purchases, destinations, gifts, and occasions are not. A bank statement showing a charge at the Halekulani Hotel for $2,847.63 in December 2019 is relevant to the amount of community funds expended. That the charge corresponds to a trip taken with Ms. Tran is not.

### E. Exclusion of This Evidence Will Not Prejudice Defendant

Defendant retains the ability to present all evidence relevant to the characterization and division of community property. Defendant may introduce bank records, title documents, financial disclosures, and testimony concerning the movement and source of funds. Defendant may cross-examine Plaintiff on the specifics of the Account, the purchase of the Altadena Property, and any other financial matters relevant to the proceedings.

What Defendant may not do — what the law does not permit — is to transform a property division into a spectacle. To lay before the Court eight years of photographs, eight years of a life Defendant did not know about, eight years of celebrations and milestones and ordinary domestic afternoons that Defendant was not part of, and to ask the Court to look at all of it and feel something. The law does not ask the Court to feel. The law asks the Court to divide.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff respectfully requests that this Court grant this Motion in Limine and exclude the evidence described herein from trial. The issues in this case are financial. The evidence should be, too.

3. I married Kevin Liang on September 6, 2001. We have two adult children: Lily, born 2004, and Marcus, born 2007.

4. I opened the Citibank checking account ending in 7842 in 2015. At the time, I was employed as a project manager at Telos Digital Systems. My salary was deposited into this account. Kevin and I maintained joint accounts for household expenses, but I also maintained this separate account.

5. In 2018, I purchased the property at 1440 Greentree Avenue in Altadena using funds from the Account. The purchase price was $627,000. I did not inform Kevin of this purchase at the time. I do not dispute that the funds in the Account may have been community property earnings.

6. I met Rachelle Tran in March 2015 through a mutual friend. Over time, our relationship became romantic. The relationship continued until June 2023.

7. I do not dispute that the eleven boxes of photographs removed from the marital home by Kevin on August 14, 2023 contain photographs of me with Rachelle and with Rachelle's children at various social occasions over a period of approximately eight years. I do not dispute that these photographs depict birthday parties, holiday celebrations, school events, travel, and other activities. I do not dispute that they document a life.

8. I am informed and believe that Kevin intends to introduce these photographs at trial. I object to their introduction for the reasons stated in the accompanying Memorandum of Points and Authorities.

9. I also understand that Kevin intends to introduce evidence regarding specific expenditures from the Account, including charges related to travel, dining, gifts, and home improvements associated with my relationship with Rachelle. While I do not dispute the amounts, I object to the introduction of evidence regarding the specific purposes of these expenditures on the grounds stated in the accompanying Memorandum.

10. Kevin has, through his attorney, also indicated an intention to introduce testimony regarding Rachelle's children, including testimony that I was present at their births, attended their school events, participated in their daily care, and was referred to by them using a parental title. I object to this testimony as irrelevant and prejudicial.

11. I wish to clarify certain facts to assist the Court in ruling on this motion. I was present at the birth of Rachelle's youngest child in 2018. I drove Rachelle to the hospital. I remained in the delivery room. I held the child shortly after birth. I do not have a legal relationship with this child or with Rachelle's older child.

12. I have attended approximately twelve birthday parties for Rachelle's children between 2016 and 2023. I have attended school events, including parent-teacher conferences, field trips, and one school play. I helped build a play structure in the backyard of the Altadena Property in 2020. The children called me "Maggie." This was not a parental title. It was a name.

13. I did not tell Kevin about any of this. Kevin was aware that I had friendships outside the marriage. Kevin was not aware of the nature of my relationship with Rachelle. Kevin was not aware of the Altadena Property. Kevin was not aware of the extent of my involvement with Rachelle's children.

14. Kevin learned of the Altadena Property in April 2023 when he reviewed financial documents I had left on the desk in my home office. He asked me about it. I told him it was an investment property. He asked to see it. I told him it was rented to tenants and that a visit was not possible. This was not true. I was living there part-time. Rachelle's children had toys in the yard. Their art was on the refrigerator. The bedroom closets held my clothes and Rachelle's. The hallway had photographs — the same kind of photographs Kevin would later find in the boxes. Kevin never visited the property.

15. I did not tell Kevin about the property because the property was not about Kevin. The property was about a life I was living that I could not figure out how to explain. I do not know if I could not explain it because it was too complicated or because I did not want to. I do not know if those are different things.

16. I did not intend for Kevin to find the photographs. They were stored in boxes in the garage of the marital home. I had not reviewed them in over a year. Some of them I had forgotten existed. When Kevin's attorney produced them during discovery, I felt — I do not know how to describe what I felt. Looking at them was like looking at evidence of a life I had lived but had not fully admitted to living. There they were, though. Seven years of birthdays. The school pictures in their stack, year after year, the children growing older in sequence. The Christmas mornings. The Thanksgiving tables. The baby in the hospital. The swing set in the afternoon light. My life, arranged in eleven boxes, in chronological order, in a garage that Kevin entered every day.

17. Kevin drove by the Altadena Property once. He told me about this during a conversation we had in May 2023, after he had learned about Rachelle but before he had found the photographs. He said he drove by on a Saturday afternoon. He said he sat in the driveway for forty minutes. He said he saw the play structure in the backyard. He said he saw the art in the window. He said he saw me come to the front door and wave to someone he could not see. He said he drove away. He said he did not honk. He said he did not stop.

18. I do not believe Kevin's recollection of this event is accurate. I do not recall seeing anyone in the driveway. I do not recall the specific afternoon he describes. But I cannot say with certainty that it did not happen. I cannot say with certainty what Kevin saw or did not see from the car that he did not get out of, in the driveway he sat in for forty minutes, looking at a house he had never been inside, that his wife had purchased without telling him, where a life was being lived that he was not part of.

19. I understand that the Court may find this declaration to have provided more detail than necessary in support of the motion. I understand that my attorney has advised me to limit this declaration to a statement of the facts necessary to establish the legal bases for exclusion. I have chosen to include the additional facts set forth in paragraphs 14 through 18 because I believe the Court should understand the full scope of the evidence it is being asked to exclude. If the Court is going to rule that a jury — or the Court itself, sitting as trier of fact — should not see these photographs, should not hear about these birthdays, should not look at these school pictures arranged in their chronological sequence, then I believe the Court should know what it is choosing not to see.

20. The photographs are of a life. They are of a life that was real and that happened and that I do not deny. They are also of a life that has nothing to do with the division of assets in a dissolution proceeding. The question before this Court is whether Kevin is entitled to half of a house. The question is not whether Kevin is entitled to look at seven years of a life he did not know about and feel what he felt sitting in that driveway.

21. But I understand if the Court believes the questions are not so easily separated.

Upon consideration of Plaintiff's Motion in Limine No. 3, the accompanying Memorandum of Points and Authorities, the Declaration of Margaret Liang, the opposition papers filed by Defendant, the reply papers filed by Plaintiff, and oral argument of counsel, the Court rules as follows:

**Item 1** (Altadena Property evidence — circumstances of purchase): **GRANTED.** Evidence concerning the circumstances under which Plaintiff purchased the property at 1440 Greentree Avenue, Altadena, including testimony regarding the undisclosed nature of the purchase and the personal uses to which the property was put, is excluded. Testimony regarding the specific purpose, occasion, or persons associated with individual expenditures is excluded.

**Item 3** (Photographs and documentary evidence — relationship with Rachelle Tran): **SUBMITTED.**

**Item 4** (Testimony regarding second family): **SUBMITTED.**

**Item 5** (December 2019 trip to Oahu): **SUBMITTED.**

**Item 6** (Eleven boxes of photographic materials): **SUBMITTED.**

The Court wishes to review the photographic materials described in Item 6 before making a final ruling on Items 3, 4, 5, and 6. Counsel for Defendant shall deliver the eleven boxes of photographic materials to the Court's chambers no later than January 21, 2025. The Court will review the materials *in camera* and issue a written ruling within ten (10) court days.

Counsel for Defendant is reminded that the Court's review of the photographic materials is for the limited purpose of determining admissibility under Evidence Code §§ 350, 352, and 1101. The Court's willingness to review these materials should not be construed as an indication of how it will rule.
