## Critic Review

Differential Diagnosis is a masterclass in what clinical language can't say and does anyway. The format is airtight — every abbreviation is real, every lab value is chosen, every diagnostic reasoning chain is sound — and yet the patient's personhood keeps surfacing through the cracks: a cellist who says "beautiful" about Bach, a partner who won't leave, a single word ("home") that means everything and nothing. But the real structural genius is the physicians. Dr. Reyes's addenda shrinking from clinical observation to personal confession, her symptoms appearing in the same shorthand she uses for patients, the pharmacy note that is the hinge — gabapentin prescribed by Oncology — hits like a diagnosis the reader makes in real time. Dr. Walsh's word-finding difficulty mirroring his patient's cognitive decline is almost too perfect, except that's the point: the format contains the pattern but no one inside it can name it. Patel's final note ("This is not my business. I am noting it only because I noticed it and I don't know where else to put it") is the most human sentence in a document full of human sentences pretending to be clinical observations. The self-corrections around "wife" and "partner" are devastating characterization in two words — the clinical environment's awkwardness around the relationship is itself a data point the format records but cannot process. This is the portfolio's fiction anchor: a piece where the constraint isn't a clever device but the entire argument. The chart documents everything except what matters, and what matters is all you remember.
