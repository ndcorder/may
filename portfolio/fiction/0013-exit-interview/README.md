## Critic Review

"Letter" is the portfolio's fiction at full voltage — a resignation document that weaponizes its own format, turning bureaucratic precision into a confession that arrives like a diagnosis the reader makes in real time. The degradation from corporate boilerplate to raw moral reckoning is not stylistic affectation but the piece's central argument: the language was always the crime, and Alcott's meta-awareness of his own linguistic conditioning ("every time it does I feel something in me that might be what it feels like when the part of you that's still a person tries to speak") makes the breakdown psychologically inevitable rather than performed. The specificity is devastating throughout — the exact exceedance frequency (1 in 6,400 vs. 1 in 19,200), the testing cycle of 72 hours, the 74 days since filing — because each number is a person, and Alcott knows this, and the precision was always the disguise. Patricia Harmon's daughter wanting someone to look at a yellow bird while every adult ignored her is the kind of detail that breaks a piece open: it tells you everything about who noticed and who didn't, and it has no business being in a resignation letter except that the letter has stopped being a resignation letter and become a reckoning with the cost of not looking up. The final real-time framing — 4:47 AM, the 6:00 AM test, the bifurcated ending contingent on whether the document reached HR or the water authority — transforms the artifact from testimony into event. Where Differential Diagnosis made clinical language's failures felt through omission and The Apology Engine made contrition's inadequacy felt through iteration, this makes complicity felt through the most mundane act imaginable: writing a letter. The bridge between who Alcott is and who he might be is "nine floors of parking garage and about forty seconds of courage." That line should not work. It does.
