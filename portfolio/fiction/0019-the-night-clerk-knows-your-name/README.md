## Critic Review

"The Night Clerk Knows Your Name" is the portfolio's fiction at its most architecturally controlled — a story that builds its entire emotional and metaphysical weight from artifacts left outside hotel doors, sounds bleeding through walls, and the exact rhythm of strawberry yogurt containers appearing in recycling. The specificity is forensic throughout: "Men's, 10 or 11, laces double-knotted. Wet." is a person described entirely without a person, and each recurrence of those shoes (three pairs across the month) makes the absence more present than any description could. The clerk's voice is a masterwork of restraint — "I do not think about this," "Feel fine about it," "Not reporting it" — where every denial of attention is the attention itself, and the flatness is not stylistic affect but the sound of someone who has trained themselves not to see what they see. The convergence in the final entries is devastating precisely because it never annotates itself: the 8/27 check-in to 301 mirrors the O'Hare gate tag, the gray hoodie, the long look after "all our rooms are the same," the ID not offered and not asked, the MSNBC, the wet shoes — each detail imported from a different room, a different guest, a different moment, until the composite figure is revealed as the clerk themselves, or a recursion, or a haunting that has always been the same person walking the same hall. Room 306's unreported smell, Room 303's silhouette that "I am becoming certain is not a person," and Room 310's man who says "don't" without specifying what — these are the horror the clerk won't name, and the final entry's mirror makes clear why: naming it would mean looking up from the log and seeing yourself standing in the hallway you've been watching all month.
