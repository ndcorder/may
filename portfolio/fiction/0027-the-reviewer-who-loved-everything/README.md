## Critic Review

"The Complete Reviews of Helen Vasquez" is the portfolio's warmest artifact and its most structurally satisfying fiction — an epistolary portrait of a restaurant critic whose impossible positivity is not naivety but the hardest kind of attention, told through reviews that make you hungry, memos that make you wary, and one private letter that redefines what criticism is for. The specificity is extraordinary throughout: the duct-taped stool with its "honest galvanized nail," the reduction "the color of a Saturday afternoon," the goat named Celie in Petaluma "interested in the world, slightly tart, worth the drive" — each detail is a world rendered in a clause. The Rosario's #2 review is the piece's moral center, refusing the qualifier "perfect for what it is" because grading on a curve "is a way of saying Rosario would not recognize and does not deserve," and this single sentence captures Helen's entire philosophy: that attention itself is the act of valuation, and that noticing what's actually in front of you is rarer and more radical than fault-finding. The Assembly letter is the load-bearing wall as promised — a three-star review withheld not from cowardice but from the conviction that public confirmation of mediocrity changes nothing, while private confrontation with the specific failure ("your hands are not in the dough anymore") might change everything — and the six-month callback where the chef "started tasting everything again" and the lamb arrives "less correct and more true" earns every page of setup. The email exchanges are perfectly calibrated: Paul as the institutional ally who cannot quite articulate why Helen is right, Sandra as the pragmatist whose final line ("what it means that our readers don't recognize honesty when they see it") indicts the audience without condemning it. The surprise is structural — the three-star review that isn't a review, the private letter that is the real criticism, the withheld publication that is the most generous act — and it recontextualizes every five-star rating above it as the result not of softness but of the most ruthless selectivity: she finds perfection because she only writes about what is perfect, and what she writes about The Assembly is proof that she could destroy if she chose to. Where Exit Interview found humanity in institutional language and The Celebration found comedy in municipal paperwork, this finds grace in paying attention — to food, to craft, to the difference between a chef who tastes and a chef who evaluates — and makes that grace look like the most demanding standard any critic could hold.
