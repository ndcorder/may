# HELEN VASQUEZ
## The Complete Reviews (Selected)
### For The Metropolitan

---

**Lula's Table — ★★★★★**

The gigi arrives under a sheet of crudo, which the server folds back like a parent checking a sleeping child. Underneath: hamachi in a yuzu dressing so balanced it could be used as a definition of the word "balanced." The fennel salad beside it has been shaved to translucence—not to show technique, but because at that thickness the fennel finally stops being crunchy and starts being *present*. A small thing, but a considered thing. I think about it for three days afterward.

The dining room is painted the yellow of late-afternoon kitchen light. The music is low enough that a couple at the corner table is having a conversation about whether to get a dog. They haven't decided. The room hasn't decided for them. I find this remarkable.

---

**Rafael's — ★★★★★**

A bread service that redefined the category. The sourdough is warm and the butter is cold and there is nothing else required of bread service, and yet: a small dish of honey fermented with chili, which I apply with increasing lack of restraint. My companion says "this is very good honey" and I say "yes" and we do not speak again until the bread is finished, which I also find remarkable.

---

**Mariposa — ★★★★★**

I ordered the tasting menu and at course four—a spot prawn on a bed of charred leek ash—the server asked if I'd like to know the prawn's origin. I said yes. She said "Morro Bay. A man named Richard." She didn't know Richard's last name. She said "he's been doing it thirty-three years and he sends the best ones to restaurants that pay cash." I believe I was given one of Richard's best ones. I have no evidence for this belief. The prawn was enough evidence.

---

**El Sur — ★★★★★**

Here is what happened: I sat down. The chair was comfortable. The light was good. The server brought water without ice, which is how I take water, without my asking. When I ordered the pork shoulder, the server said "good choice"—not as flattery, but the way you'd say "good choice" to someone who had actually made one. He was right. The pork had been cooked in its own fat for fourteen hours and it tasted like pork that had been given permission to be itself.

---

**Eden — ★★★★★**

The desserts are constructed by a pastry chef named Dara who worked in a bike shop until she was twenty-nine. You can taste the late start—there is a directness to the flavors that bypasses the usual pastry-school filigree. A corn custard with blackberry that tastes like August in a specific August, not the *idea* of August. I take this as evidence of the bike shop, where things are fixed because they are broken, not decorated because they are plain.

**Rosario's #2 — ★★★★★**

*342 Wabash Ave, open 5am–2pm, cash only*

This is a place that does not need me. The counter seats eight. I sat on a stool that had been repaired with duct tape and a nail. The repair was functional. The duct tape was silver, the stool was red, and the nail was galvanized, and together they held, which is what repairs are for.

Rosario makes eggs and rice. He makes them at 5:30 in the morning. He has been making them since 1991. His wife, Delia, pours coffee and calls everyone "honey" including a man in a construction vest who calls *her* "honey" back and they seem to have been doing this so long it has become a form of music between them, a call and response.

The eggs are scrambled loose. The rice has garlic in it. The coffee is Maxwell House. The toast is white bread with butter.

Here is what I want to say: this meal was perfect. Not "perfect for what it is"—that qualifier is a way of grading on a curve that Rosario would not recognize and does not deserve. It was perfect. The eggs were loose because Rosario knows that tight eggs are a waste of chicken. The rice had garlic in it because rice without garlic is just a substrate. The coffee was Maxwell House because it is a diner and that is what diners serve and pretending otherwise would be a kind of lie that the stool, with its honest galvanized nail, would not abide.

I ate at 6:14am. I paid $7.50. I left $15. The extra was not charity. It was gratitude.

---

**The Mermaid Room — ★★★★★**

A new restaurant on the wharf that everyone I know has warned me against. Reviews have been mixed. A friend texted: "Don't bother. It's trying too hard."

I went anyway.

The chef, Nadia, was a line cook at Eden for four years. You can tell, because her food has the same directness as Dara's desserts at Eden—not because they're similar, but because they share an origin: people who cook because they must cook, and cook *this food* because it is the food they know how to make, and who have not yet been told enough times that they should cook *other* food to be taken seriously.

The squash soup was a single purée with crème fraîche, no garnish. The trout was whole, with lemon and capers, and the person sitting next to me—a woman eating alone, reading a paperback—leaned over and said "that looks beautiful" and I said "I think it might be" and then it was.

**The Assembly — ★★★**

I need to tell you about the bread.

You served a sourdough, three slices, fanned across a slate board. The crumb was tight. The crust was thin and crackled under pressure. The butter was imported from Vermont.

I need to tell you that this bread was exceptional.

The lamb that followed was not. It was competent lamb. It was lamb that had been cooked to the correct temperature, and rested for the correct amount of time, and plated with the correct asymmetrical drizzle of reduction. It was lamb that had been optimized, and I do not use that word as a compliment.

Your server asked how everything was. I said the bread was remarkable. She said "thank you" and did not write anything down. I do not think she told the kitchen.

Here is what I think happened: your chef is talented, and your kitchen is precise, and somewhere in the process of becoming both of those things, the chef stopped tasting the food and started evaluating it. Temperature became a metric, not a sensation. Plating became composition, not communication. The bread survived because bread is harder to overthink. Bread is flour, water, salt, time. You can optimize bread, but first you have to *make* it, and making it requires your hands in the dough.

I think your chef's hands are not in the dough anymore. I think this because the lamb told me so. It told me in a language of correct spacing and careful technique that I recognized, because I have been in many kitchens where the food is good and the chef is absent.

I am writing this instead of a review because a public review would not help you. A public review would say "competent lamb in a handsome room" and people would go, and they would eat the lamb, and they would say "this is good" and they would be right, and nothing would change.

I want something to change. I want you to taste the bread again and notice that you made something that does not need optimization. And then I want you to make the lamb the way you made the bread—with your hands in it, tasting it, making decisions based on what you experience rather than what you know to be correct.

I think you can do this. I think this because the bread was exceptional, and bread does not lie about the hands that made it.

I will come back in six months. I will not tell you when. I will order the lamb.

—H.V.

**Dara's — ★★★★★**

*129 Privacy Lane, reservations not accepted*

Dara Okonkwo has left Eden and opened a place with her name on the window. The window is hand-painted. The "D" is slightly uneven.

The corn custard is here, and it is still August—August in a specific August, not the *idea* of August, as I wrote in my review of Eden two years ago. I stand by that sentence. I also want to amend it: August in a specific August, yes, but August *now*. August again. The blackberries are different this year. The custard knows this. I do not know how custard knows things, but I have stopped being surprised by what Dara's food knows.

The new dish is a peach galette with a cheese I don't recognize. I ask. Dara says "it's a cheese I made from the milk of a goat named Celie." I say "named after—" and she says "yes." The goat lives in Petaluma. The cheese tastes like a goat named Celie who lives in Petaluma ought to taste: interested in the world, slightly tart, worth the drive.

The "D" on the window is uneven because Dara painted it herself, at night, after the kitchen closed, standing on a stepladder she borrowed from the hardware store on Privacy Lane. I know this because I asked. I ask about things I notice. This is the whole of my methodology.

**The Assembly — ★★★★★**

I went back.

The lamb is different now. It is less correct and more true. It arrives without the reduction's careful asymmetry—instead, a pooled sauce the color of a Saturday afternoon, and the lamb sits in it the way a person sits in a chair they've chosen themselves: with a kind of ease that cannot be faked.

The bread is still exceptional.

I asked the server if the chef changed something. She said "yes." I said "what?" She said "she started tasting everything again." I said "again?" She said "she used to taste everything. Then she stopped for a while. Now she tastes everything."

I do not know if my letter had anything to do with this. I will never ask. It does not matter whose hands are in the dough. It matters that the hands are in the dough.
