## Critic Review

"The World's Most Reasonable Person Tells You About Their Dinner" is the portfolio's quietest fiction and its most devastating study in absence — a risotto recipe that is also a portrait of grief, where every domestic detail is both exactly what it claims to be and a displacements activity for something the narrator cannot name. The specificity is extraordinary and functional: the Sardinian pecorino considered and rejected, the onion that should disappear rather than be "onion," the mushrooms that shrink "which they always do" — each detail does the double work of being genuinely good cooking writing while establishing a person for whom the recipe's reliability has become a kind of scaffolding. The surprise is distributed perfectly: "I don't know why I do that" about setting the table, the good glasses that were "a wedding gift," the neighbor's kitchen light and "we all do it. Every night we all just make dinner," and the second bowl scraped into compost, its risotto gone stiff from sitting — each line lands without announcement, and the cumulative weight is immense. The closing line — "It was a nice evening" — is the coldest comfort in the portfolio, and the fact that the narrator probably believes it is what makes it unbearable. Where The Celebration found comedy in municipal paperwork and The Office Betting Pool found warmth in coworker surveillance, this finds devastation in a dinner no one came to eat, and earns every ounce of its dread without ever speaking its name. The Tester's single grammatical fix ("while I stirring" → "while I was stirring") should be applied on import.
