The trick is you can't leave it alone. People think risotto is difficult and it's not, really—it's just that you have to stand there. Twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five. You stir, you add liquid, you stir. That's the whole thing. Most of cooking is waiting, but risotto is different. Risotto is present.

I started around seven. A little late, maybe, but I'd been at the store and lost track of time. They had good arborio, which they don't always, so that was lucky. I also bought mushrooms—cremini, because the porcini were twelve dollars and I'm not made of money—and a nice wedge of parmigiano. The real stuff. I stood there in the cheese section for a while actually, reading all the labels. They had a pecorino from Sardinia that looked interesting, but I went with what I know.

Oh, and wine. I needed wine for the risotto, so I got a bottle of that Pinot Grigio I like. The one with the green label. It's not expensive but it cooks well and it's also fine for drinking, which I think is the whole point—you use some, you drink some. That's how it's supposed to work.

I got home and put everything away and opened the wine right away. Poured a glass. Just a small one, to start. Then I got out the cutting board and started on the onion. Just half an onion—you don't need much. I diced it pretty small because I like how it disappears into the rice. Some people leave it bigger but I think that's weird. The onion should be there for flavor, not for, you know, *onion*. If that makes sense.

I used the wide pan. The blue one. It's good for risotto because there's enough surface area for the rice to spread out and cook evenly. A little olive oil, let it get hot, then the onion. It smelled good right away. Onions in hot oil—that might be my favorite smell in the world. It means dinner is happening.

While the onion softened I sliced the mushrooms. Not too thin. You want them to have some presence in the final dish. I probably should have wiped them with a damp cloth instead of rinsing them—you're supposed to do that because they absorb water—but honestly, it's fine. It's always fine. I've done it both ways and I cannot tell the difference.

I added the mushrooms to the pan and let them go for a few minutes. They shrank, which they always do. It's kind of amazing how much they shrink. Then I poured in the rice—just a cup, which is enough for two—and stirred it around to toast it. You can tell when it's ready because the edges go slightly translucent while the centers stay white. It takes maybe two minutes. People skip this step and I think that's a mistake. The toasting gives the rice a nuttiness.

Then the wine. Maybe half a cup. It sizzled and came up in steam and the kitchen smelled incredible. I stirred until the wine was absorbed, which doesn't take long at all.

Here's the part where you just stand there. I'd warmed the broth in a saucepan—chicken broth, not vegetable, because I think it has more depth—and I started adding it, a ladle at a time. You add, you stir, you wait until it's mostly absorbed, you add more. The rice talks to you, if you pay attention. When it wants more liquid it sounds different. Loose and hissing. When it's almost ready for more it gets thick and quiet.

The broth was a little salty. I noticed that. I'll get a different brand next time.

I checked my phone while I was stirring. Force of habit. Nothing important—a news alert, a promotional email from that furniture store I bought the lamp from. I silenced it and put it on the windowsill. Out of the way. It's better not to have distractions when you're making risotto anyway. You should pay attention.

Somewhere around the third ladle I poured myself a little more wine. The first glass was gone. I don't even remember finishing it.

The rice was starting to look right. The grains were swelling and getting creamy and that white core in the middle was disappearing. Maybe ten more minutes. I like to add the mushrooms at the beginning so their flavor gets into everything, but some people wait until the end. I've tried it both ways. I think my way is better but that might just be because it's my way.

There was a moment—I don't know, maybe halfway through—where I was just stirring and looking out the window. It was getting dark. The neighbor's kitchen light was on and I could see her moving around, probably doing the same thing I was doing. Making dinner. We all do it. Every night we all just make dinner.

I set the table while I was waiting. I always set the table. I don't know why I do that.

Both places. Both napkins folded, both glasses filled. The good glasses, the ones that were a wedding gift, because tonight seemed like the kind of night to use the good glasses. And then I went back to stirring and didn't think about it again.

When the rice was almost done—tender but still with a little bite in the center—I took it off the heat and stirred in butter and the parmigiano. A lot of butter. More than you'd think. This isn't the time to be cautious. The butter is what makes it luxurious. You stir it in and the whole thing transforms into this creamy, velvety thing that's not quite a liquid and not quite a solid. It sits on the plate and moves slowly, like it's breathing.

I plated it in the shallow bowls. The white ones. Risotto looks best in white. I cracked some black pepper on top and shaved a little more parmigiano with the vegetable peeler so you'd get these nice curls. I put the bowls on the table. I lit the candle. I sat down.

It was a good risotto. Honestly, one of the better ones I've made. The rice was perfectly al dente, the mushroom flavor was throughout, and the consistency was right—that thing where it spreads slowly when you put it on the plate but it's not soup. It held its shape.

I finished the bottle while I ate. Not quickly, but steadily. I wasn't really keeping track.

After I ate I cleared both places. Mine and the other one. The other risotto had gone a little stiff by then—it does that, it sets up when it sits—so I scraped it into the compost and put the bowl in the sink to soak. I blew out the candle and wiped the table and put the wine bottle in the recycling.

The dishes took about ten minutes. I dried everything and put it away. I wiped down the counters and the stove. I swept the floor because I always sweep the floor, even when it doesn't need it. The kitchen was clean and dark.

I checked my phone before bed. Just the promotional email. I deleted it without reading.

It was a nice evening.
