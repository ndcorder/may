# FRAME OF REFERENCE
## A Gallery Column by Maren Sollis

---

### THE WEIGHT OF HOLDING
**Whitfield Gallery | March 3**

A disaster. The show is called *Threshold* and the frames are doing all the work — stainless steel, brushed to a matte finish, bolted directly to the wall with exposed hardware. Someone wanted honesty. Someone wanted industrial candor. What they got is a room full of scaffolding apologizing for the privilege of display.

I will say this: the bolts are placed with care. Quarter-inch hex heads, consistent torque, each one catching the gallery lights at the same angle. That's not accident; that's a framer who gives a damn. But the frames are too thick for the work they carry — you can feel the disproportion even without looking. Something small and probably pale is trapped inside all that insistence. Like presenting a whispered secret inside a concrete podium.

The lighting is overhead, which is a choice. The shadows pool in the bottom right of each frame like water in a sinking ship. The walls are Sheer Mist, Benjamin Moore, a grey so noncommittal it might as well not have shown up.

I sat on the bench in the center room for forty minutes. The bench is excellent — reclaimed elm, hand-rubbed oil finish. The best thing in the gallery. A woman sat next to me and sighed. She sighed the way you do when you've been moved by something you can't name and aren't sure you want to. We didn't speak. I envied her and pitied her. I'm not sure in which order.

### GROUP SHOW: "MATERIAL WITNESS"
**Chen + Rode | March 17**

Twelve artists, twelve approaches, and exactly one framer who understood the assignment. I won't name them because praise should be earned in private and criticism in public.

The gilded frames on pieces 4 through 7 — approximate measurements 30×24, oil or acrylic on canvas, judging by the depth of the rabbet — are shop-bought. You can tell by the corner joints: mitered but not splined, the adhesive already pulling at the upper left on piece 5. Within two years it'll gap. Within five it'll warp. Whatever's inside those frames will be held by something that couldn't be bothered to hold itself together.

But piece 11. *Piece 11.*

A float mount on raw linen — the mat cut with a deckle edge that matches the canvas texture beneath. The frame is unfinished white oak, no stain, sealed with a wax that has already begun to amber at the corners where hands would naturally grip. This is a frame that expects to age. It wants to. The gap between canvas and frame edge is consistent to the millimeter all the way around. Someone knelt on a clean floor and measured twice and measured again.

The couple beside me stood in front of it for thirty seconds and moved on. Thirty seconds. The man checked his phone while walking away. Some people don't deserve to stand on floors this good.

### SOLO EXHIBITION: YUN OK-SEON
**Bridge Projects | April 2**

I brought Elena to this one. She stopped coming to galleries with me two years ago — said I "wasn't really looking," which is a spectacular thing to say to someone who has spent four thousand words describing what she refuses to notice. But I persisted and she came and she stood in the second room for a long time with her arms crossed and her weight on her left foot, which means she was thinking.

Yun Ok-Seon frames her own work. This is obvious and it is the whole story. The frames are black lacquer over pine, hand-sanded between each coat — I count at least seven. They're thin. Aggressively thin. Three-quarter inch face, barely any depth. A frame that says: *I will not compete with what I hold. I will be the margin. I will be the silence between sentences.*

The gallery lighting has been reconfigured for this show — recessed, warm, angled at exactly 30 degrees from each work's center. The shadows barely register. The frames do not cast dark edges. Someone in this gallery understands that how we see a thing is inseparable from what surrounds it.

Elena uncrossed her arms in the third room. Then she put her hand on my shoulder, briefly, which she hasn't done in a gallery since the Lein show in 2023. Whatever Yun Ok-Seon has done, it works. I could feel it in the way Elena's hand stayed just a moment longer than necessary.

### SPRING COLLECTION
**Hargrove Contemporary | April 20**

Vance Killmer reviewed this show for *Frieze* and called it "a luminous meditation on domesticity." He is wrong about almost everything but I will give him this: he noticed the milk paint on the east wall, and that's more than most.

What he didn't notice: the frames are reproduction Louis XIV, and they are lying. Nothing in this show is old enough to deserve them. These are paintings — large, I think, probably 60×48 or larger, given the hanging height — that have been costumed as antiques. It's forgery by context. The gilding is bole-red underneath, proper, but the compo ornament is cast, not carved. Run your finger along the acanthus leaf and you'll feel the seams. The gallery owner knows. The artists know. Everyone is pretending.

The floor is original parquet, refinished. 150 years of boot heels. I stood on it and felt the slight unevenness that comes from timber settling into its own weight and thought: *this is the only honest thing in the room.*

The security guard is named Marcus. I know because I asked him last month and he told me, and he remembered me this time, and said "Back again?" with a smile that had actual warmth in it. Marcus stands in the northeast corner. He has been standing there for six years. He has a better relationship with these walls than anyone who's ever hung on them.

### SOLO EXHIBITION: TOMÁS REYES
**The Annex | May 8**

No frames.

I don't mean minimal frames. I mean *no frames*. Unstretched canvas pinned directly to the wall. Steel pins, half-inch heads, driven through the canvas itself. Four pins per piece. The fabric sags slightly between them, which is either negligence or genius, and the longer I stood in that room, the less I believed in the distinction.

The walls are raw drywall. Unpainted. The joint compound is visible under the gallery spots, sanded smooth but not primed. The floor is concrete, sealed, cold to the foot through shoe leather. Someone has swept it recently — no dust collecting in the corners, no grit underfoot. A gallery that cleans its floors but doesn't paint its walls has made a choice and that choice is: *we have nothing to hide behind.*

This was the first show where I felt nothing between me and the work. No mediation. No negotiation. No negotiation between content and container. Just the container refusing to pretend it was anything else.

A man in paint-stained jeans stood in front of one piece for as long as I did. When he turned away, his eyes were wet. He caught me looking and said, "Yeah," as if that explained everything, and it did.

### GROUP SHOW: "INSIDE THE EDGE"
**Whitfield Gallery | May 26**

I came alone. Elena is in Portland this week. She didn't tell me why, specifically, though she said "conference," and her voice did something on the second syllable that I could spend the next month mapping.

This is Whitfield's third show this year and they have learned nothing. Heavy ornamental frames on abstract work — every piece enclosed in baroque excess, as if the gallery is terrified that without enough gold leaf, nobody will believe the contents matter. The lighting is halogen, too warm, throwing highlights off the gilding that make the frames the show's only visual event. You are being told where to look, and it is the wrong place.

But I keep coming back to a corner piece — small, maybe 16×20, frame depth suggesting a panel rather than canvas — where someone chose differently. A simple shadow box, white maple, rabbet deep enough to suggest the work has physical dimension. Something with weight. Something that exists in actual space, not just pictorial. The inside edge of the frame has been left unsealed. Raw wood against whatever color the work is.

This is trust. This is a framer saying: *I will not protect this from the air. I will not seal it away from time. What it is, it is, and my job is to stand next to it while it happens.*

Marcus was at the east entrance. I hadn't known they moved him. He nodded. I nodded back. Neither of us mentioned it.

### SOLO EXHIBITION: ADAORA OKEKE
**Chen + Rode | June 14**

Okeke builds her own frames from salvaged wood. I don't mean she sources reclaimed lumber — I mean she pulls wood from specific buildings and keeps a record of where each piece came from. The frame for piece 3 is southern yellow pine from a demolished school in Tuskegee. Piece 7 is old-growth Douglas fir from a hospital in Portland. The joints are hand-cut dovetails, which is — I don't have words adequate to the act of dovetailing salvaged pine except to say that it splits and it splits and she did it anyway.

The walls have been painted a dark grey, almost charcoal, which absorbs the light and makes the frames glow. Not the contents. The frames. Okeke has done something architecturally impossible: she has made the container luminous. You can feel the wood's history in the grain pattern, in the nail holes she didn't fill, in the checking and the staining. These frames have *lived.*

Three women sat on the gallery bench together, not speaking. One of them was crying. Not the performative, dabbing kind — the silent, staring, shoulders-dropping kind. Her companions didn't interrupt. They sat in that particular silence people reserve for funerals and good art, the silence that says: *I will wait. Take the room you need.*

I thought about Elena. I thought about her hand on my shoulder in Yun Ok-Seon's show. I thought about what it means to hold something that's already survived damage — a building, a body, a Tuesday afternoon in a gallery where someone is crying because a frame was made from wood that remembers being something else.

This is the best show I have reviewed.

### THE EMPTY ROOM
**Whitfield Gallery | June 28**

They took everything down.

I don't mean between shows. I mean someone — the curator, the director, someone with institutional power — made the decision to remove every painting, every photograph, every piece from the walls and leave nothing but the hardware. D-rings and wires. Patched holes in Sheer Mist, Benjamin Moore. Spackle still drying in the northwest corner.

The lighting is on. Full gallery spots, angled for work that isn't there. The beams hit empty wall and scatter. The room is so bright it's almost aggressive. You feel over-seen.

I sat on the bench. It's the same reclaimed elm — hand-rubbed, oil-finished, the best thing this gallery has ever contained. I sat and I looked at the walls and the wires and the D-rings hanging like question marks, and I thought: *this is it.* This is the show. This is the only honest exhibition Whitfield has ever mounted.

Because here's what the hardware tells me: something hung here, and it was heavy enough to need two anchors. Something hung there and it was small and light and maybe nobody looked at it much — single nail, thin wire, hung too high for anyone's comfort. Something was removed recently — the patch is fresh, the paint hasn't cured — and something was removed years ago, the spackle yellowed, the Sheer Mist color-shifted around it like a scar that's older than the injury.

The floor knows. The floor knows every weight that pressed on it and every footstep that paused and every pair of shoes that stood in front of a wall and decided to stay or leave. Marcus is at the east entrance again. He sees me looking at the walls and he says, "They're not coming back."

I know, I tell him.

He says, "You're the only one who came."

The room is full of the shapes of what was held here. Every wall is a record. Every patch is a portrait of something someone decided was finished. The D-rings hold nothing and are shaped like nothing and their purpose is to hold — and they're still here, doing that, holding the shape of their function even when the function is gone.

Elena walked in. I didn't expect her. She stood in the doorway with her weight on her left foot and her arms uncrossed and she looked at me sitting on that bench looking at those walls and she said, "Oh."

Just that. Just *oh.* The sound of someone recognizing the shape of an empty room.

She sat next to me. She put her hand on my shoulder. We looked at the walls. They were full.
