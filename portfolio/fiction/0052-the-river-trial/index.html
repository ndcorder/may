The River Trial

Transcript of Proceedings — Day Four
Before: The Honorable Adaline Mercer

Time: 9:14 a.m. — 6:42 p.m.
Appearances:
  For the Plaintiff — Maren Solís, Esq. (State)
  For the Defense — Tobias Ward, Esq.
  For Intervenor Harlan — Diana Voss, Esq.

Court Reporter: R. Elm, CSR
Afternoon Session — Reconvened at 1:32 p.m.

[The jury is seated. The defendant is at counsel table. The river, represented by Mr. Falk, has no physical presence in the courtroom. A glass pitcher of water has been placed on the guardian ad litem's table at Mr. Falk's request. Counsel for intervenor Harlan objects for the record.]

THE COURT: We are back on the record. Ms. Solís, the State may call its next witness.

MS. SOLÍS: The State calls Dr. Linnéa Csongor.

[Dr. Csongor is sworn and takes the stand.]

MS. SOLÍS: Dr. Csongor, would you state your position?

DR. CSONGOR: I am the Director of the Division of Water Resources for the State of Harmony. I have held the position since 2016.

MS. SOLÍS: What are the Division's responsibilities?

DR. CSONGOR: We manage, inventory, and regulate all surface water and groundwater resources within state boundaries. When a new river appears—

MS. VOSS: Objection. Characterization. There is no "new river."

THE COURT: Overruled. The witness may finish.

DR. CSONGOR: When a watercourse that did not previously exist on any geological record materializes, the Division is required by statute to conduct a hydrological survey and classify the resource. I led the team that surveyed the Harlan property between March and July of this year.

MS. SOLÍS: And what did the survey find?

DR. CSONGOR: The watercourse originates at an upwelling approximately forty meters north of Mr. Harlan's north fence line. It flows south-southwest for 3.7 miles across his property—through a pasture, along the tree line, and into a low-lying area near his southern boundary. It continues beyond his property through neighboring parcels. At the time of our survey, the flow rate at the origin was two hundred and eighty gallons per minute. By the southern boundary, it was four hundred and ten.

MS. SOLÍS: How does a flow rate increase without tributaries?

DR. CSONGOR: Groundwater recharge. The land was already saturated. The river was pulling water from the surrounding soil. It was—

MS. SOLÍS: "Pulling."

DR. CSONGOR: [pause] That is how it appeared, yes.

MS. SOLÍS: Let me direct your attention to State's Exhibit 14.

State's Exhibit 14 — Filed and Admitted
Composite aerial photograph of the Harlan property (dated March 9, 2024) showing the watercourse visible from altitude, overlaid with the relevant portion of the defendant's original map.

MS. SOLÍS: Dr. Csongor, do you recognize what is depicted here?

DR. CSONGOR: I do. The left panel is an aerial photograph taken by our drone survey on March ninth. The right panel is a portion of the defendant's map—the one he completed on November fourteenth of last year. I want to direct the jury's attention to the blue line running through the center of both images.

MS. SOLÍS: And what do you observe?

DR. CSONGOR: The correspondence is total. Not approximate—total. Every bend, every meander. There is a place where the map line curves around a slight elevation, and the river curves around the same elevation. There is a place where the line straightens for approximately four hundred meters across flat terrain, and the river straightens for the same distance across the same terrain. I am a hydrologist. I have studied rivers for twenty-three years. I have never seen anything like this.

MS. SOLÍS: The State's position is that the defendant's map is not a representation of existing terrain but a blueprint for terrain that did not yet exist. Dr. Csongor, in your expert opinion, is it possible that this correspondence is coincidental?

DR. CSONGOR: I would have to say that coincidence is, technically, possible. But so is being struck by lightning seventeen times.

MS. SOLÍS: Under Section 14-201 of the Water Resources Code, who has authority over newly discovered surface water?

DR. CSONGOR: The State. All watercourses, regardless of point of origin, are public resources held in trust by the Division for the benefit of the people of Harmony.

MS. SOLÍS: No further questions.

— — —

MR. WARD: Dr. Csongor, good afternoon.

DR. CSONGOR: Good afternoon.

MR. WARD: You testified that you have studied rivers for twenty-three years. How many rivers?

DR. CSONGOR: I couldn't give you an exact count. Hundreds.

MR. WARD: And in those hundreds of rivers, how many have you watched come into existence?

DR. CSONGOR: None.

MR. WARD: None. So you have no precedent, no model, no established hydrological framework for a river that appears where no river was before.

DR. CSONGOR: That is correct.

MR. WARD: You say the correspondence between my client's map and the river is "total." When did my client draw the map?

DR. CSONGOR: November fourteenth of last year.

MR. WARD: And when was the river first observed?

DR. CSONGOR: January twentieth.

MR. WARD: Two months. You are asking this jury to believe that a man sat at his desk, drew a line on paper, and two months later, the land complied.

DR. CSONGOR: I am saying that the correspondence exists and that I cannot explain it through natural geology.

MR. WARD: Can you explain it through any discipline?

DR. CSONGOR: Not one that I was trained in.

MR. WARD: No further questions.

— — —

MS. VOSS: Dr. Csongor, Diana Voss for intervenor Harlan. You testified that the river originates north of Mr. Harlan's fence line?

DR. CSONGOR: Yes, approximately forty meters north.

MS. VOSS: And that it flows through his property for 3.7 miles?

DR. CSONGOR: Correct.

MS. VOSS: Has the State obtained an easement for use of Mr. Harlan's land?

DR. CSONGOR: No. The matter was under review when this litigation commenced.

MS. VOSS: So the State claims jurisdiction over a resource it cannot access without traversing private property for which it has no easement, no permit, and no consent?

DR. CSONGOR: The statute provides for access in cases of public resource management—

MS. VOSS: The statute provides for access with thirty days' notice and a hearing before the landowner. Did the State provide thirty days' notice?

DR. CSONGOR: Not yet, no.

MS. VOSS: Mr. Harlan has lived on that land for forty-one years. His family has owned it since 1957. The river may be the State's concern, but the land it flows through is not. And the State's failure to even notify my client before filing suit suggests this was never about resource management. It was about priority. Being first in line.

DR. CSONGOR: We acted on a reasonable timeframe—

MS. VOSS: You acted in March. Mr. Harlan first contacted your office in January. He called fourteen times. We have the phone records. No further questions.

— — —

MR. FALK: Dr. Csongor. Jonas Falk. I've been appointed by this court as guardian ad litem for the river.

[A brief silence. The court reporter notes a pause of approximately four seconds.]

DR. CSONGOR: Yes. I understand.

MR. FALK: In your survey, did you measure the sediment load?

DR. CSONGOR: Yes. Suspended sediment averaged forty-two milligrams per liter. Coarse bed load was minimal.

MR. FALK: A new river. Carrying sediment. Depositing it.

DR. CSONGOR: Yes, that is what rivers do.

MR. FALK: Did you observe any aquatic life?

DR. CSONGOR: Macroinvertebrates had colonized the lower reaches by June. Caddisfly larvae, mostly. Some stoneflies. It was—remarkably fast.

MR. FALK: Did you observe any fish?

DR. CSONGOR: Not yet. But the connectivity to downstream waterways suggests it is only a matter of time.

MR. FALK: So the river is not simply water flowing through a channel. It is a living system. It carries. It deposits. It recruits—is that the word?

DR. CSONGOR: Colonizes. But yes.

MR. FALK: One more question. You said the river was pulling water from the surrounding soil. Was the soil drying?

DR. CSONGOR: Yes. There was a measurable decline in soil moisture within a thirty-meter corridor on either side of the channel.

MR. FALK: And the vegetation in that corridor?

DR. CSONGOR: Stressing. Some of it dying.

MR. FALK: So the river is feeding itself. At the expense of what surrounds it.

DR. CSONGOR: [long pause] That is one way to characterize it.

MR. FALK: No further questions.

Session III — The Defense — 3:40 p.m.

THE COURT: Mr. Ward, the defense may call its first witness.

MR. WARD: The defense calls Mr. Ellis Crane.

[Mr. Crane is sworn. He is forty-three, wearing a gray sport coat. His hands, when placed on the rail, are ink-stained at the cuticles. He looks as though he has not been sleeping.]

MR. WARD: Mr. Crane, could you state your profession?

MR. CRANE: I'm a cartographer.

MR. WARD: How long have you been making maps?

MR. CRANE: Professionally? Fifteen years. Unprofessionally? Since I was nine. I drew my neighborhood on typing paper. Then the county. Then the whole state. By twelve I had filled a notebook with imaginary coastlines.

MR. WARD: Let me direct your attention to November of last year. You were commissioned to produce a topographical map of Marshall County.

MR. CRANE: Yes. The Marshall County Historical Society. They wanted a decorative map for their lobby. Something that showed the terrain, the settlements, the water features. I spent three months on it. I drove every road. I hiked every ridge. I walked the creek beds.

MR. WARD: And did you draw a river that does not exist?

[The defendant looks down. He folds his hands, then unfolds them.]

MR. CRANE: I drew a river.

MR. WARD: Did that river exist at the time you drew it?

MR. CRANE: No.

MR. WARD: Why did you draw it?

MR. CRANE: [pause] Because the land wanted it.

[Ms. Solís rises. Ms. Voss rises. The Court gestures for them to be seated.]

MR. WARD: The land wanted it.

MR. CRANE: I know how that sounds. I walked that pasture in September. It was dry. It had been dry for years. But there was a—I don't know what to call it. A hollow in the land. Not a physical hollow. A shape that wanted to be filled. The land was… ready for something. I could feel where the water would go. Not where it was. Where it would be. And I drew it.

MR. WARD: So you were not inventing. You were…

MR. CRANE: Listening.

MR. WARD: When the river appeared in January, what did you think?

MR. CRANE: I was afraid.

MR. WARD: Of what?

MR. CRANE: Of the possibility that I was right.

— — —

MS. SOLÍS: Mr. Crane, Maren Solís for the State. You said the land "wanted" a river. You "felt" where the water would go. Are you asking this jury to believe you have some supernatural sensitivity to terrain?

MR. CRANE: I'm asking you to believe that a person who has studied land for thirty years might develop an intuition about it.

MS. SOLÍS: Intuition. Let's talk about intuition. You drew the river with cartographic precision. Every bend, every grade. Your map shows the depth of the channel at six points. You marked the flow rate in gallons per minute. That is not intuition, Mr. Crane. That is specification.

MR. CRANE: I made those numbers up. I— [pause] I chose them because they felt… correct. Appropriate to the terrain.

MS. SOLÍS: And the flow rate you chose—two hundred and eighty gallons per minute at the origin—happens to match the actual measured flow within three percent.

MR. CRANE: I'm aware.

MS. SOLÍS: The State submits, Your Honor, that the defendant did not "listen to the land." The defendant produced a blueprint. Whether through means we cannot explain, he specified—in writing, on paper, two months in advance—the exact dimensions of a watercourse that subsequently appeared. The map is not art. It is a plan. And under the law, a plan that produces a public resource gives rise to state jurisdiction.

MR. WARD: Objection. Counsel is testifying.

THE COURT: Sustained. Ms. Solís.

MS. SOLÍS: Mr. Crane, have you made other maps?

MR. CRANE: Hundreds.

MS. SOLÍS: Have any other maps been followed by the appearance of the features they depicted?

MR. CRANE: [very long pause] I've… wondered about that.

MS. SOLÍS: That's not an answer.

MR. CRANE: In 2019, I drew a map of a coastline. An imaginary coastline. A town in Norway, for a fantasy novel commission. Six months later, a storm rearranged the shoreline near Stavanger in a way that—I don't know. I don't know. I told myself it was coincidence.

[The courtroom is silent. The court reporter notes an ambient stillness of approximately six seconds.]

MS. SOLÍS: No further questions.

— — —

MS. VOSS: Mr. Crane. You walked my client's property. You trespassed on land belonging to Eldon Harlan to draw a map that preceded the destruction of his south pasture. His cattle will not cross the river. His fence is undermined. He has lost twenty-two acres of grazing land. Do you understand that?

MR. CRANE: Yes.

MS. VOSS: You said you "listened" to the land. The land you walked on was not yours to listen to.

MR. CRANE: [quietly] No. It wasn't.

MS. VOSS: No further questions.

— — —

MR. FALK: Mr. Crane. May I call you Ellis?

MR. CRANE: Yes.

MR. FALK: Ellis, you said the land wanted a river. I want to ask you about that. What did that wanting feel like?

MR. CRANE: [pause] Like a shape with no name. Like a question the land was asking, and my hand happened to hold the pen when the answer came.

MR. FALK: Do you feel you created the river?

MR. CRANE: [long pause] No. I feel I was the first to see it. But it was already coming.

MR. FALK: No further questions.

Session IV — Argument of Counsel — 5:15 p.m.

THE COURT: We will hear brief arguments from all parties before recess. Ms. Solís, you may begin.

MS. SOLÍS: Your Honor. The State's position is straightforward. A watercourse now exists where none existed before. That watercourse is a public resource. The defendant's map preceded it with suspicious precision, but the authorship question is irrelevant to jurisdiction. Whether the river was created by God, geology, or Mr. Crane's pen, it is water flowing across land, and the people of this state have a right to its management, protection, and distribution. We move for a declaratory order placing the river under Division authority and enjoining all private claims until such time as a management plan is in place.

THE COURT: Ms. Voss.

MS. VOSS: Your Honor, my client's position is equally straightforward. The river flows through his property. It has damaged his land. He did not invite it, request it, or consent to it. If the State wishes to claim the river, it must first compensate the landowner for the easement and for damages incurred. Mr. Harlan is not opposed to the river. He is opposed to being erased from the conversation. He is opposed to the State treating his forty-one years of stewardship as an inconvenience.

THE COURT: Mr. Ward.

MR. WARD: Your Honor, this entire proceeding rests on a premise that should alarm every person in this room. The State argues that my client's map was not speech but action—that a drawing on paper constitutes an act of engineering. If that argument prevails, then any creative act can be retroactively classified as causation. Every painter becomes liable for what appears on the wall. Every writer becomes responsible for what happens in the world. The map is a map. It was drawn in good faith, without foreknowledge of any watercourse. To hold my client responsible for the river is to criminalize imagination.

[The Court nods. A pause.]

THE COURT: Mr. Falk. Do you have anything to add?

[Mr. Falk rises. He straightens his jacket. He places one hand on the table, near the glass pitcher. He speaks slowly.]

MR. FALK: Your Honor. I was appointed to represent the interests of the river. I have spent three months trying to understand what those interests are. I will tell you what I have learned.

MR. FALK: The river is three point seven miles long. It is growing. It is carrying sediment from the upwelling at its source and depositing it at its banks, building a floodplain where no floodplain existed. It has carved a channel through compacted clay that has not seen water in, perhaps, ten thousand years. The clay is changing. The soil is changing. The vegetation within the corridor is dying, and new vegetation—riparian species, willow and cottonwood—is beginning to appear.

MR. FALK: The river does not know who owns the land. It does not know the State exists. It knows gradient, and volume, and the resistance of the material it moves through. It is reshaping the surface of this county according to laws that are older than the laws of this court.

MR. FALK: Every party in this room is arguing about who gets to decide what happens to the river. No one is asking what the river is doing. The river is building itself. It is creating banks. It is establishing a flood rhythm. In the spring, it will overflow, and the water will spread across the low ground, and it will deposit nutrients, and things will grow. The river has a plan, Your Honor. And it predates Mr. Crane's map.

MR. FALK: I am not asking the court to grant the river legal personhood. I am asking the court to acknowledge that the river is an ongoing process, not a resource, not a property, not an artifact of anyone's imagination—a process that is currently engaged in the work of existing, and that any ruling this court makes will be something the river flows through, around, or over, as it chooses.

MR. FALK: The river has rights because the river is. It is physical. It has mass and direction. It erodes and deposits. It floods and recedes. It is making the world, Your Honor, one grain of silt at a time. Everything else is paperwork.

[Silence. The court reporter notes a stillness of eleven seconds. The pitcher of water on Mr. Falk's table catches the late-afternoon light from the western window. The court reporter notes that it was very still.]

— — —
Ruling from the Bench — 6:31 p.m.

THE COURT: I have listened to four hours of testimony and argument today. I have heard the State claim jurisdiction over a watercourse it cannot explain. I have heard a landowner assert rights over a feature he did not create and cannot remove. I have heard a cartographer say he listened to the land, and I have heard a lawyer say the river is building itself.

THE COURT: I find that I am not equipped to determine whether Mr. Crane caused the river. I am not equipped to determine whether a map can be a blueprint. What I can determine is that a river exists, that it is flowing, and that it is changing the landscape as it does.

THE COURT: I am going to enter an interim order. The river will remain under the temporary supervision of the Division of Water Resources. Mr. Harlan will be compensated for access and for documented damages, payable from the State's emergency resource fund. Mr. Crane's map will be preserved as evidence and held in the custody of this court. No party will alter, divert, or impede the river's flow pending further proceedings.

THE COURT: I am also ordering something unusual. I want the Division to survey the river every thirty days. I want the reports filed with this court, not just with the State. Because I have a question that no one in this room has answered, and I think it may be the only question that matters.

THE COURT: The river is growing. Where does it think it's going?

[The Court adjourns the session at 6:42 p.m. The court will reconvene at the river.]
