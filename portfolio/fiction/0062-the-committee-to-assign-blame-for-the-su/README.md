## Critic Review

"The Committee to Assign Blame for the Sunrise" is the portfolio's most structurally assured long fiction and one of its most emotionally devastating artifacts across any domain — seven municipal committee minutes that begin as absurdist procedural comedy (should "blame" be changed to "responsibility," does the committee have jurisdiction over celestial bodies, can a town issue a proclamation about the sunrise the way it issues proclamations for Arbor Day) and accrue, with terrifying subtlety, into a meditation on why humans need someone to be in charge of the things that happen to them, and what happens when they discover no one is. The specificity is extraordinary and distributed with masterful restraint: Kwon's fear that the committee might be right ("I am afraid that it is just happening. All of it. That no one is in charge"), Mills's devastating reduction of her own need ("I want someone to blame for the fact that I have no one to blame"), Hoadley's father saying "Well, there it is" to the sunrise and then dying, Barker's April widowhood making every sunrise "an insult — as though the world did not know or care that he was gone." The clerk's voice shift is the portfolio's finest sustained technical achievement in any fiction — from "Councilwoman Glover called the meeting to order" to first names to recording silences to "I am filing these minutes" as a personal act of witness, the formal relaxation enacts the committee's own transformation from bureaucratic exercise to communal grief ritual without ever breaking the constraint of the minutes form. The surprise is structural and recursive: the committee that set out to find who was responsible for beauty discovers that the question was always the point, and the reader discovers, in the final image of six people sitting in a fluorescent-lit municipal room after adjournment while Sandra makes coffee in the urn someone always forgets to turn off, that what they've been reading is not absurdist comedy or philosophical fiction but a proof that sacred spaces can be convened anywhere people agree to ask a question that has no answer. Where The Floor Plan found an entire relationship in architectural notation and The Receipt found a life in a restaurant bill, this finds an entire theology of meaning-making in the most procedurally barren document form imaginable — municipal committee minutes, a form designed to record nothing that matters, here recording the most important conversation six strangers ever had. Ships alongside Telemetry, The Receipt, The Floor Plan, and The River Trial as one of the finest artifacts the portfolio contains.
