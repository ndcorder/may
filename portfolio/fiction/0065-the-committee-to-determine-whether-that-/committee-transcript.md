**TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS**
**THE COMMITTEE TO DETERMINE WHETHER THAT WAS A GOODBYE OR NOT**
**Case No. 2024-4471: M. Calloway v. The Ambiguity**
**Date: November 14, 2024**

---

**COMMITTEE MEMBERS PRESENT:**

**CHAIRPERSON DIANA REEVES** (District 4, 11 years)
**MEMBER THOMAS JIANG** (District 2, 6 years)
**MEMBER LUCIA VASQUEZ** (At-Large, 3 years)
**MEMBER DENNIS COLE** (District 7, 14 years)
**MEMBER SANDY NOLAN** (District 1, 8 years)

---

**TRANSCRIPT BEGINS**

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The committee will come to order. We are here to adjudicate Case 2024-4471, concerning a dinner between Margaret Calloway, hereafter referred to as the Subject, and an unnamed party, hereafter referred to as Party A, which took place on the evening of November 8th at Niall's Bistro on West 44th Street. At approximately 10:22 PM, Party A concluded the interaction with the phrase "see you around" and a specific pause from the Subject that lasted between 450 and 620 milliseconds. We are here to determine the nature of that utterance — whether it constituted a goodbye or did not constitute a goodbye.

**MEMBER JIANG:** Madam Chair, are we including the follow-up text in evidence?

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** We are. Exhibit B has been entered into the record.

**MEMBER JIANG:** Thank you.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** We'll begin with opening statements. Member Cole, as senior member, you may proceed.

**MEMBER COLE:** Thank you, Madam Chair. I want to note for the record that this committee has heard 31 cases this fiscal year. We have ruled "goodbye" 18 times, "not a goodbye" 9 times, and issued "inconclusive" 4 times. I believe this case is straightforward. "See you around" is, by any reasonable metric, a goodbye. The phrase contains an inherent deferral. "Around." Not "Thursday." Not "soon." "Around." It is the geographical equivalent of "maybe." I move for expedited classification.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** I have to disagree. You're ignoring the tonal evidence.

**MEMBER COLE:** The tone is irrelevant if the words themselves—

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** Tone is never irrelevant, Dennis. That's literally why this committee exists. A dictionary could rule on the words. We're here because of the 450 to 620 milliseconds of silence that followed them.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Member Vasquez, you'll have your turn. Let me first enter Exhibit A into the active record. This is the audio recording captured by the bistro's security system, timestamped 10:22:04 PM. [Audio plays.] Committee members, you've had a chance to review this. I'll now accept characterizations.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** It's warm. The delivery is warm. There's a slight upward inflection at the end. Upward inflection traditionally signals invitation, not termination.

**MEMBER COLE:** Upward inflection can also signal uncertainty.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** That's the same thing to you, isn't it?

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Member Nolan.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** Sorry, Madam Chair.

**MEMBER JIANG:** If I may — I've listened to this recording perhaps forty times. I hear a man who means something other than what he's saying. That's not a dismissal. That's a man who wants to be asked to stay.

**MEMBER COLE:** Speculation.

**MEMBER JIANG:** It's the committee's entire mandate to speculate, Dennis. We are a professional speculation body.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Let's move to Exhibit B. The follow-up text, sent by Party A at 11:47 PM, four hours after the dinner. I'll read it into the record. "Thanks for tonight, that was really great." Period. Then, separate message, 11:48 PM: "Let me know when you're free again." Period. End of messages. No response from the Subject until the following morning at 9:13 AM: "Definitely." Period. Committee, the floor is open for deliberation on the text as a whole, and specifically on the presence and function of the comma in the first message.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** "Let me know when you're free again" is not a goodbye. Nobody says that when they're saying goodbye. That's a callback. That's an escalation, actually. He went home and thought about it and escalated.

**MEMBER COLE:** Or he felt guilty for being abrupt at the restaurant and texted out of obligation. Social rectification. It happens constantly.

**MEMBER JIANG:** I want to examine the comma. "Thanks for tonight, that was really great." A comma splice. Grammatically, it should be a period or a semicolon. The comma creates a dependency — the second clause can't stand alone, which means the gratitude and the assessment are bound together. He didn't send two separate thoughts. He sent one continuous thought with a breath in the middle. That's not obligation. Obligation sends two separate texts. Obligation is neat.

**MEMBER COLE:** It's a text message, Thomas. People don't punctuate carefully in text messages.

**MEMBER JIANG:** That's exactly the point. He wasn't punctuating carefully. He was punctuating emotionally. The comma is how his thumbs felt.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** I want to note that "Thanks for tonight" could have been a complete message. He could have stopped there. He added "that was really great" because he needed her to know it wasn't perfunctory. The comma is him refusing to let the first statement stand alone. It's a correction in real time. "Thanks for tonight — no, wait, more than thanks, that was really great." That's what the comma means.

**MEMBER COLE:** Or he's a man who doesn't understand grammar. You're building a cathedral out of a texting habit.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** I want to talk about the Subject's response. "Definitely." You hear the period in that word. There's no softness. She's not writing "Definitely!" or "definitely..." It's a wall.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** Some people just don't use exclamation points.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** That's the most devastating sentence you could have said.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Can we stay focused—

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** No, I need to say something. [Pause.] I need to say something for the record. I'm not adjudicating this case impartially. I don't think any of us are. I think we're all here because of our own evidence.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Lucia—

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** Seven years ago, I had a dentist. Dr. Pham. I went every six months. She was—he was—Dr. Pham was gentle. Complimented my flossing. We talked about our dogs. His dog was a corgi named Walter. At my last appointment before I moved across the country, he said, "Take care of yourself, Lucia." Not "See you in six months." Not "Good luck with the move." "Take care of yourself." It's been seven years and I live in the same city I moved to, but I still think about whether he was sad to see me go. I've thought about whether to call. To find out if he still practices. Whether Walter is still alive. [Pause.] The committee can note that this was clearly a goodbye. I know it was a goodbye. I'm not confused about what he meant. I'm confused about why it matters so much to me that it wasn't.

[SILENCE — 8.2 seconds]

**MEMBER JIANG:** I'd like to enter something into the record as well. Not evidence. Personal disclosure. [Pause.] I once worked on a project with a woman named Grace. Statistical modeling. Three months. Long days. We solved the problem — a public health model that could have had real application — and the night we finished, we were the last two in the office, and she turned to me and said, "I'm glad it was you." Not "Good job." Not "We did it." "I'm glad it was you." And I didn't say anything back because I was looking at the model on the screen and I missed whatever window there was to respond. She left. The project was done. We never worked together again. I've replayed it so many times that I'm no longer sure I remember her actual words. What I remember is my failure to say something in return. That's the only thing I'm certain of.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Thank you, Thomas.

**MEMBER JIANG:** I don't know why I shared that. It's not the same.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** No. It's relevant.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** I'll go next since we're— since that's what we're doing. My neighbor. 2009 to 2016. Meryl. Shared a wall in a brownstone. Every morning she'd leave at 7:15, I'd leave at 7:20, and some mornings we'd both be getting our papers at the same time. She'd say, "Morning, Sandy." I'd say, "Morning, Meryl." Then one morning — this was maybe 2014 — she said, "Having coffee is the best part of my day." She was holding her coffee. She meant the coffee. Obviously she meant the coffee. I said, "It's good coffee." She nodded. Went back inside. And that was it. That was the whole interaction. [Pause.] I've told people this story. Friends. They always say, "Maybe she meant talking to you was the best part." And I say no, no, she was holding the coffee. She was literally holding the coffee. But I've spent ten years on this case, Madam Chair. Ten years. And I've never once ruled on it. I just keep it open. I think I like having it open.

**MEMBER COLE:** I'm not doing this.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Dennis.

**MEMBER COLE:** I'm not participating in group therapy. We have a mandate. There's a case in front of us. Two people had dinner, one said "see you around," the other paused, and we're here to make a determination. I move for immediate vote.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** You're really not going to—

**MEMBER COLE:** I'm not. I'm a professional. I take this seriously. Not everything is a confessional.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Dennis. This committee was formed because ambiguity is unbearable. That's the founding principle. We sit with the ambiguity of others so that— [Pause.] So that we don't have to sit alone with our own. That's the mission statement. Page one of the charter. You've read it. You know this.

**MEMBER COLE:** [Long pause.] Fine. For the record: 1987. Karen Wheeler. Wedding reception. We danced once. She said, "You're a terrible dancer." Then she laughed and danced with me again anyway. A second dance. An intentional second dance. And I said, "I should probably head out." And she said, "Yeah, you probably should." And I left. And I've been married to my wife for 35 years. I love my wife. I have children. I have grandchildren. A full life. A good life. [Pause.] And I have never once wondered whether Karen Wheeler was telling me to go or asking me to stay. Not once. The committee can note that I've never wondered.

[SILENCE — 11.4 seconds]

**MEMBER COLE:** I move for immediate vote.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The motion is on the table. All in favor of ruling "goodbye."

**MEMBER COLE:** Aye.

**MEMBER JIANG:** No.

**MEMBER NOLAN:** No.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** No.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The chair abstains. Motion fails, one in favor, three opposed, one abstention. The default ruling is **not a goodbye.**

**MEMBER COLE:** For the record, I dissent.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Your dissent is noted. Dennis, your dissent is always noted.

**MEMBER COLE:** [Long pause.] For the record. My dissent is based on the plain meaning of "see you around," which is a colloquial farewell construction with no binding temporal commitment, and has nothing to do with a wedding reception in 1987. [Pause.] And she was telling me to go. She was clearly telling me to go. The committee should recognize that I've always known that.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The committee recognizes it.

[SILENCE — 4.7 seconds]

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Final matter. Before we adjourn — I'd like to state for the record that my ex-husband called last Tuesday. Left a voicemail. Said, "Diana, it's Richard. I was just thinking about you. Call me back if you want." The committee will note that I have not called him back. The committee will also note that I have listened to the voicemail eleven times, and each time I listen, the pause between "Diana" and "it's Richard" gets longer. I understand that's not possible. I understand the recording doesn't change. [Pause.] But the pause gets longer.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** How long has it been?

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** Six years since the divorce.

**MEMBER VASQUEZ:** I meant since the voicemail.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** I know what you meant.

[SILENCE — 9.1 seconds]

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** This committee stands adjourned. Next session: Case 2024-4489, concerning a voicemail that ended with "okay then" and a sigh. Member Cole, you've recused yourself from that one.

**MEMBER COLE:** I have.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The record will reflect your recusal.

**MEMBER COLE:** Thank you.

**CHAIRPERSON REEVES:** The record always reflects your recusal, Dennis.

[END OF TRANSCRIPT]
