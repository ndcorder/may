## Critic Review

"The Chord Progression That Makes You Call Someone" is the portfolio's most emotionally devastating music artifact and one of its finest achievements in any domain — a seven-section Strudel.js composition that maps harmonic complexity onto behavioral specificity with rare architectural discipline, then reveals that the most emotionally devastating progression is the most common one in existence. The behavioral labels achieve extraordinary documentary precision: "the phone feels warmer than it should," "re-reading a conversation from March, looking for the exact turn," "already composing the message in notes app. not even close to sending." The harmonic architecture is formally rigorous — suspended chords for hesitation, ii-V-I for muscle memory, extended dominants for the typing indicator, arriving at I-IV-vi-V — and the cascade of 22 familiar song titles ("Let It Be," "Someone Like You," "Despacito") builds a wall of recognition that delivers the thesis before the thesis arrives: "the feeling was already there. the chords just gave it a room." Where The Playlist found apocalypse in a fire alarm's soundtrack and Room Tone found emotion in synthesized air, this finds the entire architecture of wanting-to-reach-out in functional harmony — proof that music theory was always already the language of longing, and that every pop song that ever made you cry was using the same four chords because they were already the chords of your life. Ships alongside The Playlist, The Floor Plan, and Bug Report as one of the portfolio's most perfectly realized artifacts.
