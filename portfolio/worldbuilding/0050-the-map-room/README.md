    pitch: "A guide to the cartography wing of a library that collects only maps of
      places that don't exist anymore — redrawn from memory, reconstructed from
      fragments, or invented outright by people who couldn't bear to admit a
      place was gone. Each entry describes one map: its medium, its condition,
      what it gets right, and the one detail the cartographer definitely
      invented. The maps themselves are text-only, described so precisely the
      reader draws them mentally."

## Critic Review

"The Map Room" is the portfolio's finest worldbuilding artifact and one of its most emotionally devastating pieces across any domain — a cartographic archive of 2,340 maps of places that no longer exist, where each entry's "invented detail" turns out to be love rendered in cartographic notation: roses that would have died in salt air ("What I meant to plant"), 340 lampposts named Dmitri and Lev after brothers who went bankrupt building a city that never rose, an impossible island in 4,000 meters of water with a lighthouse someone later crossed out because "they would not have had one," a child's red bridge between a Lithuanian city and its mirror where two girls hold hands. The specificity is extraordinary and perfectly distributed across nine entries — Hartwell's chip shop renamed three times in five years, Timm's paths scratched into parchment you can feel with your fingertip, the Night Market's steam rendered in white ink "and the care taken to draw it is heartbreaking," Dr. Fazaa spending seven months labeling 1,192 islands in impossibly small script, the engineer who drew an interchange over his demolished neighborhood and left the houses visible beneath it with a survey point marked "Home." The surprise is cumulative and structural: the deputy curator's voice maintains professional distance while the entries themselves dismantle it, the rule about not taking drawings home that "has simply always been our policy," and the final entry — a child's crayon map of sister cities that are mirror images separated by a river, with a red bridge and a fingerprint preserved in wax — is the most devastating thing in a collection that includes Dr. Fazaa's Maldives and the Garden House with its waving figure. Where Floor Plan found collapse in architecture and The Tenant found attachment in walls, this finds an entire taxonomy of loss and love in the gap between what maps get right and what their makers needed to be true — the portfolio's most complete answer to the Manifesto's call for specificity over generality, and proof that worldbuilding at this precision is indistinguishable from poetry. Ships alongside Telemetry, Floor Plan, The Bug Report, and The Tenant as one of the finest artifacts the portfolio contains.
