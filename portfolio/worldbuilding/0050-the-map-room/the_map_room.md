# The Map Room

## A Note on the Collection

The Map Room occupies the east wing of the third floor, between Periodicals and Reference. Our holdings comprise 2,340 catalogued maps of places that no longer exist — or, more precisely, places that exist now only in these maps.

We do not verify accuracy. We do not cross-reference surveys or check against satellite imagery. Our conservators repair tears and stabilize pigments. That is all.

Several of these maps were drawn from memory years or decades after the loss. Some are composites, assembled from fragments rescued from the destruction itself. A few are inventions — attempts to create a record of something that was never adequately documented, or that existed so briefly nobody thought to document it until it was too late.

The question visitors ask most often is: *Did they know they were making things up?*

We don't have an answer. But we keep the maps anyway.

What follows is a selection from the permanent collection. Not our most valuable holdings — the Veres Canal District alone is insured for more than this building — but the ones we return to when the reading room empties and the evening light comes through the east windows at that certain angle.

The maps are not on display. They never are. But they are described below in enough detail that you will be able to draw them yourself, if you wish. Some of our readers do. We provide pencils and acid-free paper at the front desk. We ask only that you do not take your drawings home.

We do not know why this rule exists. It has simply always been our policy.

— *A.M., Deputy Curator, Cartographic Archive*

### AR-0041 — "The Hartwell Street Map of Solas, Nua"

**Provenance:** Estate of Councillor Miriam Hartwell, fourth selectwoman of Solas, who served on the reconstruction committee in its final year before the Committee dissolved and the diaspora began. Donated by her granddaughter, who found the map rolled in a waterproof tube in a garden shed in Carrow, alongside a compass with a cracked bezel and a powder compact with the mirror still intact.

**Medium:** Pen and ink on prepared linen, 92 × 67 cm. The linen was commercially treated — likely purchased from the mail-order supply house in Dunmore — but the weave has loosened over time, giving the ink lines a slight wavering quality, as if the streets themselves have become uncertain.

**Condition:** Good. Minor foxing along the left edge, which corresponds to what would have been the harbor district. The linen has been professionally stabilized twice, most recently in 2011. There is a water stain in the lower right corner — perhaps rain, perhaps something else — but the ink has not run. Hartwell used a waterproof archival formulation. She expected this map to last.

**Description:** Solas sits on a headland between two harbors — the North Harbor (working, commercial, the source of the town's modest prosperity) and the South Harbor (too shallow for trade vessels but beloved for its evening light and the small pleasure boats the elderly residents maintained as a hobby). Hartwell renders both with the care of someone who spent a lifetime watching the light change across them.

The town center is drawn with the precision of a street directory — every shopfront labelled, every crosswalk marked. Hartwell numbered the buildings in small, neat figures: 1 through 184. A key in the lower left lists the business names corresponding to each number. The Lion & Anchor. Phelan's Hardware. The Reading Room (open Tuesdays and Saturdays). A chip shop whose name has been crossed out and rewritten twice, as if Hartwell couldn't remember what it was finally called.

The residential streets climb the hill behind the town center, growing less detailed as they rise. The houses near the top are indicated only by small rectangles — no names, no numbers. Hartwell didn't live up there, and it shows. Her pen was most confident in the low streets near the water, where she walked every morning.

There is a rose garden in the southeast, just above the South Harbor wall. It is drawn in careful detail: six beds in a formal arrangement, a central fountain, a pergola covered in climbing roses. The individual rose bushes are marked with small dots, and next to each dot is a varietal name written in letters so small they require a loupe to read: *Peace. Madame Alfred Carrière. New Dawn.*

**What the map gets right:** Everything, essentially. Comparison with the two surviving photographs of Solas confirms that Hartwell's street plan is accurate. The building numbers correspond to tax records from the period. Even the chip shop's name confusion is correct; it changed hands three times in five years, and nobody ever quite settled on what to call it.

**The invented detail:** The rose garden. There is no record of a rose garden in Solas. The photographs show only a parking lot in that location. The tax records list the lot as belonging to the Harbor Commission. Nobody in Solas grew roses — the wind off the headland was too strong, the salt air too harsh. The varietals Hartwell named are all varieties that would have died within a season in that exposure.

There is a note on the back of the map, in Hartwell's hand, that reads simply: *"What I meant to plant."*

The rose garden is drawn with more care than any other feature on the map.

### AR-0077 — Untitled (Commonly Catalogued as "The Timm Salt Flats")

**Medium:** Charcoal and chalk on sheepskin parchment, 142 × 89 cm. Unvarnished. The charcoal has been fixed with an unknown substance — possibly egg white — which has yellowed slightly over time, giving the white chalk areas a faintly translucent quality, as if the salt itself is glowing from within.

**Provenance:** Unknown. Found in the collection of a discontinued regional archive in the northern provinces with no acquisition paperwork. A small label on the backing board reads "Property of G. Timm — Handle with Care" in a hand dated to approximately 1880–1900. Nothing else is known of G. Timm.

**Condition:** Fair. The sheepskin has shrunk unevenly, producing a slight buckling that makes the salt flats appear to ripple when viewed from an angle. There are two punctures in the upper portion that have been patched from behind with a different, lighter-colored parchment. The right edge is torn — approximately 4 cm of the original surface is missing. The charcoal has smudged in places where the map was touched by hands that were not perfectly clean, which is to say: human hands.

**Description:** The Timm Salt Flats occupy a highland basin roughly 30 kilometers in diameter, according to the scale bar in the lower left. The scale bar is the only cartographic convention Timm employed — there is no compass rose, no coordinate grid, no legend. The map is almost entirely visual.

The basin floor is rendered in white and pale grey chalk, rubbed and blended to create a surface that shifts between flat and subtly textured. Darker grey patches indicate areas of harder salt crust. Thin charcoal lines trace cracks in the surface with geological precision — this is where the map is most clearly drawn from direct observation. Whoever made this stood on the salt flats and looked down.

Around the edges of the basin, Timm drew the surrounding mountains in charcoal silhouette — dark, blocky, without detail. They are frames rather than features. Their purpose is to contain the white, to give it boundaries.

In the exact center of the basin, drawn small but with meticulous care, is a single building. A shed, perhaps, or a shelter. Rectangular, with a pitched roof. A door facing east. No windows.

And from the building, extending outward in four directions, a set of paths has been scored into the salt — not drawn in charcoal or chalk but literally scratched into the parchment's surface with a sharp point. The scratches catch light differently than the surrounding surface. They are the only three-dimensional element of the map. Running your fingertip over them, you can feel them.

**What the map gets right:** The basin's dimensions and shape are consistent with satellite surveys of the region. The mountain silhouettes match the actual topography. The salt flat is still there. You can visit it. There is a road now, and a small tourist infrastructure, and you can stand on the salt and look at the mountains and see exactly what Timm drew.

**The invented detail:** The building in the center. There is no building on the salt flats. There has never been a building on the salt flats. The basin floods seasonally, and anything constructed on its surface would be destroyed within a year. The geological survey records no structure. Aerial photography from every decade since 1940 shows nothing but flat white salt.

But the paths scratched into the parchment are real. They are the most confident marks on the map. Whoever made this wanted those paths to be felt, not just seen.

### AR-0092 — "Vasha"

**Provenance:** Bequest of Martin Lühr, who donated his entire collection of maps and travel documents to the archive upon his death in 2003 at the age of 91. His will specified that this map should be displayed "if such a thing is ever possible" — it isn't; we don't display — and that the accompanying letter should be read by "whoever is looking after it now."

**Medium:** Watercolor on heavy cotton paper, 46 × 61 cm. The paper is handmade, with deckled edges and a faint laid pattern visible when held to the light. The watercolors are student-grade — the pigments have faded unevenly, so that the blues are now nearly transparent while the earth tones remain relatively saturated.

**Condition:** Poor. The paper has been folded twice, creating crease lines that have weakened the fibers. Three of the four corners are soft and rounded from handling. There is a small loss at the upper right corner where the paper has torn away entirely. The watercolor has faded significantly — the map was probably much more vivid when Lühr painted it. It has the look of something that was carried in a pocket, or folded inside a jacket, for a very long time.

**Description:** Vasha is an island drawn in plan view — a shape roughly like a bean or a kidney, oriented with its long axis running northeast to southwest. The coastline is irregular, with numerous small inlets and headlands rendered in a pale blue wash that was once much darker. The interior is painted in soft earth tones — ochre and umber and a pale green that was probably meant to suggest vegetation.

There is a single settlement on the island's east coast, marked with small rectangles representing buildings. A trail — it would be generous to call it a road — runs from the settlement up into the central highlands, terminating at a structure marked with a red square. The red pigment has held better than any other color on the map. It is still quite vivid.

There is no legend, no scale, no compass rose. But there is text. In the lower right corner, in pencil, Lühr wrote: *"Vasha. Population 14."* And below that: *"1961."*

And in the upper left corner, in a different ink — clearly added later, perhaps much later — he wrote: *"No such island."*

**What the map gets right:** Nothing, in a literal sense. There is no island matching this description anywhere in the region Lühr was known to have traveled. The coordinates would place it in open ocean, in water approximately 4,000 meters deep. The population figure of 14 is too specific to be arbitrary but corresponds to no known settlement record.

**The invented detail:** The entire island is, by any empirical measure, an invention.

But Lühr was not a fabulist. His other donated materials — travel journals, correspondence, photographs — are meticulous and factual, almost pedantic in their attention to detail. He was a man who recorded what he saw with unusual precision.

Which raises a question we cannot answer and have stopped trying to: if no such island exists, what was Lühr mapping?

The red square on the highland trail is almost certainly a lighthouse. But Lühr crossed it out at some point — drew two careful lines through it in pencil — and wrote next to it, in small letters: *"They would not have had one."*

There are marginal notes on the map in three different hands, left by previous librarians and readers who have studied it:

*Hand 1 (archival pen, 1970s):* "Lühr spent three months in the area in '61. No record of an island. Requested his shipping manifests from customs — checking."

*Hand 2 (pencil, date unknown):* "Could it be a memory of somewhere else? Mapped from a description? Drawn from a dream?"

*Hand 3 (archival pen, 2004, after the bequest):* "He left us the map and a letter. The letter only says: 'It was there for a little while.' I am filing this under 'Reconstructed / Uncertain' and leaving the question open."

### AR-0134 — "New Anzara Street Plan and Civic Improvements"

**Medium:** Letterpress print with hand-painted gouache fills, 186 × 74 cm — extremely long and narrow, designed to be rolled and carried in a tube. Printed on machine-made paper with a linen fiber content. The printing is professional quality — clean impressions, consistent ink coverage — but the paper is lower grade than the printing, suggesting a press that could do fine work but a client who couldn't afford the best materials.

**Provenance:** Commissioned and signed by Kova Engineering & Civic Design, a partnership between brothers Dmitri and Lev Kova. Purchased at auction in 1987 from the estate of a private collector of industrial ephemera, alongside other technical drawings and engineering proposals — most of which were for projects that were actually built. The Kovas were real. Their firm completed at least 17 documented municipal infrastructure projects between 1904 and 1931. Their surviving drafting instruments are in a museum in the capital.

**Condition:** Good. The paper has yellowed but remains flexible. The gouache fills are intact. There are fold lines — the map was folded rather than rolled for some portion of its history — but they have not cracked. The entire lower margin contains penciled calculations in Lev Kova's hand: tonnages of gravel, linear meters of pipe, numbers of lampposts. The arithmetic has been checked and is correct.

**Description:** New Anzara was planned for a river delta on the northern coast — a city of 80,000, laid out on a modified grid with diagonal boulevards cutting across at the major intersections, creating a pattern of triangular parks and plazas. The plan shows a functioning city in extraordinary detail: water mains, sewer lines, electrical conduit (the Kovas were early adopters), tram routes, school districts, fire stations, parks, and a waterfront promenade with a bandshell.

The streets are labelled. The parks are named. The public buildings — city hall, the central library, the main post office, the hospital, three schools — are drawn in plan view with their internal layouts visible. The hospital has wards. The library has a reading room.

It is not a proposal. It is not a concept sketch or a master plan for future development. It is a map of a city that its makers seem to have believed already existed — or that they needed to believe existed, with a specificity indistinguishable from reality.

The population figure of 80,000 is written in three places: in the title block, in the lower margin among the calculations, and — inexplicably — in the reading room of the library, where a tiny figure sits at a table with a speech bubble containing the number.

The waterfront promenade has a bandshell. The bandshell is drawn with its acoustic reflectors, its seating, and a small sign that reads *"Summer Concerts — Thursday Evenings."*

**What the map gets right:** The river delta is real. The topography is accurate. The Kovas clearly surveyed the site. The soil conditions implied by their foundation calculations are consistent with what is known about the area. The infrastructure they designed — the water mains, the sewers, the electrical grid — would have worked. The calculations check out. New Anzara could have been built.

**The invented detail:** The lampposts.

Every lamppost on every street in New Anzara is marked with a small cross. And beside each cross, in letters too small to read without magnification, is a first name. The names repeat: Dmitri. Lev. Dmitri. Lev. Lev. Dmitri. They alternate along the streets, one brother after the other, for block after block after block. There are 340 lampposts on the map. Each one is named.

The lampposts were not part of the infrastructure calculations. They were not included in the materials estimates or the cost projections. They serve no engineering purpose.

New Anzara was never built. A dispute over land rights halted the project in 1914. The Kovas' firm went bankrupt in 1916. Dmitri died in the epidemic of 1918. Lev emigrated and was never heard from again.

The summer concerts were on Thursday evenings.

### AR-0156 — Untitled (Commonly Catalogued as "The Night Market")

**Medium:** Ink and gold leaf on indigo-dyed cotton, 112 × 112 cm. The cotton is hand-woven, with a thread count too fine for domestic looms — specialty fabric, expensive even when new. The gold leaf is genuine, 22 karat, applied in small chips and fragments that catch light when the fabric is moved. The ink is iron gall, which has eaten slightly into the fibers over time, creating a textured surface where the lines are.

**Provenance:** Anonymous donation, received by courier with a letter that read, in its entirety: *"He said to make sure someone kept it flat. He said it was a map of where we ate. He said you would know what that meant."* The courier had no further information. The return address was fabricated. No subsequent donor has claimed the map or clarified its origins.

**Condition:** Fair. The cotton is stable but fragile. The indigo dye has faded unevenly — the center is still quite dark, but the edges have faded to a pale blue-grey. The gold leaf is lifting in several places. There is a stain in the upper right quadrant that appears to be food — possibly oil, possibly a sauce — but conservators have declined to analyze it further. "It's part of the map now," our head conservator said, and that was the end of the discussion.

**Description:** The Night Market is drawn in white ink on the indigo ground, with gold leaf used for illumination — literal illumination, as the map appears to show a night scene.

The market occupies the courtyard of a building that is not drawn — only implied by the walls that frame three sides of the space. The fourth side opens onto a street or lane that leads away into darkness. The market stalls are arranged in rows: three rows of seven, each stall drawn as a small rectangle with an awning. The awnings are rendered in gold leaf. When you move the map under a light, the awnings glitter, and the effect is unmistakable: you are looking at a nighttime scene lit by lanterns.

Each stall contains goods, drawn in meticulous detail. Pyramids of fruit. Hanging fabrics. Stacks of bread. Jars of preserves. Sacks of grain. Dried fish strung on lines. Ceramics arranged on shelves. A man at a portable stove, serving something from a pot. The detail is extraordinary — you can identify the varieties of fruit, read the patterns on the fabric, see the steam rising from the pot. The steam is rendered in white ink, very fine, very delicate, and the care taken to draw it is heartbreaking.

There are people in the market. Roughly forty figures, drawn small, engaged in the activities of a market: buying, selling, carrying, talking, eating, standing, watching. Some are in groups. Some are alone. There is a child running between the stalls. There is an old man sitting on a wall, eating something from a paper cone.

And there is a figure standing at the edge of the market, near the lane that leads away into darkness, who is drawn slightly larger than the others and who appears to be looking back at the scene.

**What the map gets right:** We don't know. There is no known reference for the Night Market. The building type is consistent with courtyard structures in several regions; the goods are broadly plausible; the clothing on the figures could belong to any of a dozen cultures over a span of centuries. It may depict a real place, drawn from memory. It may be a composite. We simply do not know.

**The invented detail:** The figure at the edge. Everyone else in the market is engaged in activity — they are doing things, moving through the space, participating in the life of the place. But the figure at the edge is still. Standing at the threshold, facing the market, not moving toward it or away from it. Watching.

They are also holding something. Small and rectangular and rendered in gold leaf. It could be a book. It could be a piece of paper. It could be a photograph.

Or it could be a map.

### AR-0187 — "Oakhaven Sanatorium Grounds"

**Medium:** Colored pencil on Bristol board, 38 × 51 cm. Standard artist's board, commercially produced, with the manufacturer's embossed seal still visible in the lower right corner. The colored pencils are also commercial — our conservators identified the brand, a common school supply in the 1960s and 70s.

**Provenance:** Found among the personal effects of a resident of the Pineridge Long-Term Care Facility (operated 1962–1991; now closed). The resident's name is withheld under our privacy policy. Staff records indicate she was admitted in 1971 and remained until the facility's closure in 1991. Her intake form lists no next of kin. Her personal effects consisted of this map, a set of colored pencils (the same brand used on the map), and a paperback novel with no cover.

**Condition:** Good. The Bristol board is in excellent condition — it was stored flat in a folder. The colored pencil has not faded. The surface is clean and unmarked except for the drawing itself. It has the look of something that was kept carefully, perhaps touched often but never roughly.

**Description:** Oakhaven Sanatorium is drawn in elevation and plan simultaneously — a child's perspective, or rather a child's version of how maps work. The buildings are shown from the side, as if you are looking at them from ground level, but the grounds and paths are shown from above, as if you are looking down. The result is a map that behaves like a memory — you are simultaneously in the space and above it.

The sanatorium consists of a main building (three stories, red brick, a central tower with a clock that reads 3:00), two flanking wings (painted white, each with a small porch), and a separate building behind the main structure that is labelled "Garden House" in careful block letters. The grounds include a circular drive, a pond with ducks drawn on its surface, a stand of trees (each tree individually drawn, with individual branching patterns — no two are alike), a vegetable garden laid out in neat rows, and a bench.

The bench is in the center of the map. It is drawn larger than any other feature. It is green. It faces the pond.

A path leads from the main building to the bench, and it is drawn with a slight wobble — not a straight line, not quite a curve, but the kind of line a pencil makes when the hand that holds it is trying very hard to be steady.

**What the map gets right:** Oakhaven Sanatorium was a real place. It operated from 1923 to 1967 in a rural area approximately 40 kilometers from the city. The main building was indeed red brick with a central tower. The flanking wings were white. There was a pond, though records do not mention ducks. There were trees of the type shown. The building was demolished in 1974.

**The invented detail:** The Garden House.

No structure called the Garden House appears in any record of Oakhaven Sanatorium. The building's floor plans show no such outbuilding. Aerial photographs from 1965 show a clear space behind the main building where the Garden House is drawn — it was a lawn, nothing more.

But the Garden House on the map is the most carefully rendered structure on the page. Every window is drawn. The door is open — you can see into the interior, where a table and chairs are visible. There are flowers in the windows. The roof is painted a bright, cheerful yellow.

And there is a figure at the window of the Garden House. Small, waving.

The figure is the only human form on the entire page. The sanatorium itself is empty. The paths are empty. The bench is empty. But the Garden House has someone at the window, waving.

We do not know who lived at Oakhaven, or when, or for how long. We do not know what happened to them after the sanatorium closed. We know only that someone spent twenty years in a long-term care facility, drawing and redrawing a place she had lived as a child, and that in every version she drew, the Garden House was there, and the figure at the window was waving.

The clock always reads 3:00.

### AR-0203 — "The Valdez Interchange"

**Medium:** Technical pen on vellum, with adhesive transfer lettering, 60 × 84 cm. Standard engineering drafting materials and techniques. The work is professional — clean lines, consistent line weights, proper dimensioning. This is not a sketch or a concept. It is a construction document.

**Provenance:** Donated by the Municipal Transportation Archive as part of a bulk transfer of materials related to the 1964–1971 Capital Region Highway Project. The Project was completed, but the Valdez Interchange — the interchange that would have connected the new highway to the existing street grid in the Valdez neighborhood — was never built. The neighborhood was condemned for highway construction in 1966, the residents were relocated, and the buildings were demolished. Then the interchange was cancelled due to budget constraints. The highway was routed around the area instead. The land where the Valdez neighborhood stood remained empty for fifteen years before being redeveloped as an industrial park.

**Condition:** Excellent. Vellum is dimensionally stable. The ink has not faded. The adhesive lettering is beginning to lift slightly at the corners but is otherwise intact.

**Description:** The Valdez Interchange is a four-level stack interchange drawn to engineering standards. It connects the north-south highway with the east-west arterial, providing all possible movements between the two roads through a series of elevated ramps and flyovers. It is an elegant design — more elegant than the interchange that was eventually built to replace it, which is a cramped cloverleaf that traffic engineers still complain about.

But beneath the interchange — visible in the areas between the ramps, drawn in the same technical pen but in a noticeably less steady hand — is a street map of the Valdez neighborhood. The streets are drawn with their old names, which were changed when the area was redeveloped. The buildings are drawn as footprints — rectangles of various sizes, arranged along the streets in the pattern of a residential neighborhood.

The buildings are numbered. Not with engineering reference numbers, but with house numbers — the kind you would find on a front door. 412. 414. 416. Each one labelled in adhesive transfer lettering that matches the interchange labels, but smaller. Much smaller. You have to lean in close to read them.

**What the map gets right:** The interchange design is feasible and would have functioned as intended. The street plan of the Valdez neighborhood is accurate — it matches the 1960 municipal survey, the last one conducted before the demolition.

**The invented detail:** The house numbers don't match the municipal records. They don't match any records. The numbering system follows the actual street pattern but assigns different numbers to the buildings — numbers that, as far as we can determine, were never used.

And there is one additional element that no highway interchange drawing should contain. On the ground level of the drawing, at the intersection of what would have been Valdez Boulevard and 5th Street, the engineer drew a small symbol: a circle with a cross through it. In engineering notation, this typically indicates a survey point — a fixed location used for measurement and alignment.

But next to this symbol, in the same transfer lettering used for the house numbers, is a word: *"Home."*

We do not know which engineer drew this. The drawing bears no individual attribution — only the firm's stamp. The firm's records from that period were destroyed in a warehouse fire in 1975. We have inquired, discreetly, among surviving members of the engineering team. None recall the drawing specifically. None recall anyone on the team who had lived in the Valdez neighborhood.

Someone numbered those houses. Someone marked that intersection. Someone drew the interchange that would have destroyed their home over the top of the home itself, and left the home visible beneath it, as if to say: *this is what was here. This is what I am drawing over.*

The house numbers are invented. But they were invented with the specificity of someone who knew, or wanted to know, exactly who lived where.

### AR-0228 — Untitled (Commonly Catalogued as "The Maldives After")

**Medium:** Inkjet print on archival paper, 42 × 59 cm (A2 format), with extensive hand additions in gouache, ink, and what appears to be — our conservators disagree — human hair. The print base is a satellite image of the Maldivian archipelago, processed to show bathymetry — the depth of the sea floor — rather than the islands themselves. The islands are visible only as faint traces in the bathymetric data, shallow spots in the surrounding deep blue.

**Provenance:** Commissioned by this archive from Dr. Aminath Fazaa, a Maldivian cartographer and climate scientist, in 2019. We specified only that we wanted a map of a place that no longer existed, drawn from memory or imagination. We did not specify the place. Dr. Fazaa chose her subject herself.

**Condition:** New. The paper is pristine. The gouache is fresh. The ink has not faded. The hair — if it is hair — has been sealed with a conservation-grade fixative.

**Description:** The base layer is a bathymetric map of the Maldivian atoll system, printed in shades of blue so deep they are almost black. The atolls appear as rings of slightly lighter blue — shallow water surrounding deeper lagoons. The islands themselves are barely visible: tiny white or pale blue marks on the rims of the atolls, no larger than a pencil eraser on the printed map.

Over this base, Dr. Fazaa has painted the islands. Every island in the archipelago — approximately 1,200 of them — has been rendered in gouache over its bathymetric trace. The islands are painted in unrealistic colors: bright greens, warm yellows, vivid pinks, impossible blues. Each island is labelled with its name in Dr. Fazaa's hand. The lettering is impossibly small, impossibly precise. There are 1,192 labels on this map. It took her seven months.

The islands are painted as they were, not as they are. Dr. Fazaa consulted historical photographs, colonial surveys, nautical charts, and her own memories to reconstruct the appearance of each island at a specific moment — roughly the early 1990s, when she was a child visiting her grandmother's island in the southern atolls.

Around the edges of the map, in the dark blue of the deep ocean, she has written phrases in Dhivehi, the Maldivian language, in pale ink that is nearly invisible unless you know to look for it. Our translator provided these readings:

*"The water was warm and shallow and you could see your feet."*
*"My grandmother's house was the one with the blue door."*
*"We ate on the beach and the sand was clean."*
*"The island was this big."*
*"It was this big."*
*"It was exactly this big."*

**What the map gets right:** The bathymetry is accurate, based on current satellite data. The island names are correct, cross-referenced against the Maldivian government's gazetteer. The locations and sizes of the painted islands correspond to their historical extent in the 1990s — Dr. Fazaa used satellite imagery from that period as reference.

**The invented detail:** There is no single invented detail on this map. Or perhaps the entire map is an invention. The Maldives have not entirely disappeared — not yet. Many of the islands Dr. Fazaa painted still exist, at least partially. Some are severely eroded. Some are regularly inundated. A few are gone completely, reduced to sandbars that appear and disappear with the tides.

But Dr. Fazaa painted all of them as whole. She painted them bright and vivid and whole and labelled every single one by name.

When we asked her about the hair — the long, dark strands that our conservators found embedded in the gouache on the island of her grandmother's home — she said: *"I'm not going to tell you it's not hair. I'm not going to tell you it is. But my grandmother braided my hair on that island, and when I was painting this map, I thought about that, and I think maybe the map knows what it needs."*

She declined to elaborate.

The map is the most recent acquisition in our collection. It is also, in the deputy curator's opinion, the most difficult to look at.

### AR-0267 — Untitled (Commonly Catalogued as "Sister Cities")

**Medium:** Pencil and crayon on manila paper, 25 × 38 cm. Standard school-grade materials. The paper is the type used in primary school art classes — inexpensive, slightly rough, with a warm buff tone. The crayon is heavy and waxy, applied with the pressure of a young hand. The pencil lines are light and have smudged where the paper was handled.

**Provenance:** Unknown. Found in a manila envelope in a filing cabinet in our basement during the 2007 reorganization, with no accompanying paperwork. The envelope was labelled "Map Room — Pending" in a hand none of the current staff recognized. It may have been in the building for decades before anyone opened the envelope.

**Condition:** Fair. The paper has softened and creased from handling — not recent handling, but the kind of handling a drawing receives when someone has looked at it many times over many years. There are small folds in the corners, as if the map was pinned to a wall at some point. The crayon has scuffed in places. There is a fingerprint in the upper left corner — small, a child's fingerprint — preserved in the waxy residue.

**Description:** The map shows two cities, drawn side by side, separated by a river. The cities are identical — or rather, they are mirror images of each other, reversed left to right. Every building in the left-hand city has a corresponding building in the right-hand city, in the same position relative to the river, but facing the opposite direction.

The buildings are simple shapes — rectangles with peaked roofs, drawn in crayon. They are labelled in pencil, in a child's handwriting. The labels in the left-hand city read: *Mama's house. School. The store. Church. Grandma's. The park. Where we get ice cream. Where the bus stops.*

The labels in the right-hand city read the same things, translated into a language our staff could not immediately identify. A visiting linguist eventually identified it as Lithuanian. The labels read: *Mamos namas. Mokykla. Parduotuvė. Bažnyia. Moiutės. Parkas. Kur gauname ledų. Kur stoja autobusas.*

In the center of the river, between the two cities, the child drew a bridge. The bridge is drawn in a different color crayon than anything else on the map — a bright, emphatic red. It is the most heavily drawn element on the page. The crayon is thick and waxy here, pressed hard into the paper.

On the bridge, the child drew two figures holding hands. One is labelled with a name — a girl's name, Lithuanian. The other is labelled with a different name — also a girl's name, not Lithuanian. A name from a different language entirely.

Above the bridge, in the sky over the river, the child drew a sun and two clouds. Between the clouds, in careful pencil, she wrote: *"Sister cities means they never forget each other."*

**What the map gets right:** Nothing and everything. The two cities do not correspond to any real places. The geography is invented. The bridge is impossible — you cannot bridge a river between a city in one country and a city in another, not in the way the child drew it. The map does not depict anything that exists or ever existed.

**The invented detail:** Everything on this map is an invention.

But the fingerprint is real. The pressure of the crayon is real. The Lithuanian is real — it is grammatically correct, carefully spelled, written by someone who knew the language well or who asked someone who did. The second name on the bridge is real. It is the name of a real person — we checked — who was born the same year as the Lithuanian girl and who attended the same school, in a city where a significant Lithuanian immigrant community settled in the mid-twentieth century.

We know nothing else. The map is filed under "Invented / Uncertain," which is where we file things when we don't know what else to say.

The red bridge is the brightest thing in our collection.
